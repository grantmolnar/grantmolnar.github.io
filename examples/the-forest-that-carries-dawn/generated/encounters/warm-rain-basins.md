# Warm Rain Basins

**Role:** necessary  
**Tags:** ecology, water, resource, moving-region

Warm rain climbs through a branching circuit that can sustain caravan drinking, rescue two mules, cool the Bearer, and still return—or be drained at a named cost.

## Opening View

Reed’s lead rope leaves the mud, climbs a root face, and disappears over the lip of a basin suspended above the party. Warm drops follow it upward. Moth’s tracks circle the steaming rim; her bell sounds from the next hollow, farther east each time. Under the water, one pale return cord lies pinched flat, and the basin is growing hotter around it.

## Encounter Material

## Situation

The Warm Rain Basins are cupped hollows carried at different heights through the moving roots. Water rises between them in beads, sheets, and capillary cords. Each basin has an outgoing path and a cooler return. Where both remain open, the water keeps a steady warmth and carries mineral traces from the ground it has touched.

The missing draft pair Moth and Reed followed this chain away from the low hollow. Their prints climb where no dry animal could. One return cord is pinched beneath a shifted root, and several basins are growing hotter as water leaves faster than it returns.

The water cools the Bearer, wets fungal cords, opens lantern stems, carries pollen, and returns through the crown. Drink from replenishing yield, borrow through a loop, or drain a branch at a cost; marked water makes the difference visible.

## What can be learned without intervention

A patient observer can establish the following facts.

- Water rises in discrete spills synchronized with root pulses.
- The western lip overflows even when the central and eastern branches remain full.
- The central branch warms lantern stems, wakes pale fungal cords, and appears as mist beneath the crown.
- The eastern branch cools roots ahead of the blackgrass burn.
- Roots accept warm rain where living old soil has been delivered and shed it from pale untreated surfaces.
- A pinched lower channel closes several lantern cups, then forces water through a different basin after one pulse.
- Successive pools warm from east to west as the Glass Waste reflection approaches.
- Moth's hoofprints follow the cool western rim; Reed's stop at each upward spill and resume during stillness.

These observations show that the basin chain is a circuit with spare capacity in one place, not an inert reservoir with a single total volume.

## The rain current

Use the circulating-rain line in `ECOLOGY-OPERATING-SHEET.md`.

- **Source:** condensation and upward dew gathered from the lower leaves and roots.
- **Path:** suspended basins, capillary cords, branch gutters, and return lines.
- **Receivers:** living roots, lantern stems, fungal cords, the blackgrass cooling shelf, the crown, and the Bearer's own cooling tissues.
- **Visible sign:** harmless markers reappear at several receivers while the temperature gradient remains steady.
- **Tolerance:** modest drinking from the western spare lip is sustainable; larger use remains borrowing only when a credible clean return reaches the circuit before the relevant stage.

The rain begins **working**. It can become strong through a restored lower return, repaired soil acceptance, and an intact eastern cooling branch. It becomes weak if one branch is pinched, drained, or late. It becomes absent only where source, path, or receiver is fully broken or fouled.

## Running the region

Before diverting a branch, answer six questions.

1. Which branch is being observed or diverted: western spare, central ascent, eastern cooling, or lower return?
2. What receiver depends on it now?
3. How much water is being moved, and for how long?
4. What clean marker will show where it goes?
5. How will warmed or used water return, if the proposal calls it borrowing?
6. What route remains for people, animals, and containers after the basins climb?

Resolve thirst, animal recovery, circuit mapping, and repair as separate operations with distinct costs.

## Safe markers

Safe markers include pollen from an opened flower, crushed red upland clay, clean charcoal dust, shell lime, temperature, harmless magic, or another non-toxic tracer. Oil, spice, soap, blood, ink of uncertain composition, and White Salt are not safe markers.

A marker placed in one basin should be checked at four points after the next spill: the intended branch, one neighboring receiver, the lower return, and the source basin. If it appears in only one place, the path is local. If it returns to the source, the party has demonstrated circulation rather than one-way drainage.

## Observation and bounded experiments

### Mark-and-trace

Place a harmless distinct marker in the western, central, or eastern branch and follow one complete spill. Central water appears in lantern stems, fungal cords, and crown mist. Eastern water spreads beneath blackgrass roots. Western overflow reaches dark living roots and returns through the lower gutter.

**Weak result:** the climbing route prevents observers from reaching every receiver before the next pulse. The path is partly established, and a missing observation becomes a reason to follow the current into another region rather than “no result.”

### Measured drinking test

Fill one skin from the western spare lip while marking the overflow level. The lip refills before the next spill without dimming the central branch. Repeatedly filling sealed barrels lowers the lip, then closes a lower cooling root.

**Weak result:** a container is lost upward or spills across sterile salt. The replenishment threshold becomes clear, but the water or route is lost and the contaminated patch must be isolated.

### Return-temperature test

Take warm water from the western lip, cool or use it, then pour it into the lower return gutter. Clean water is accepted if it remains near body temperature and free of soap, salt, blood, or oil. Very cold water stalls; hot water closes the gutter until the next pulse.

**Weak result:** the return is accepted but takes the western root branch rather than the intended central branch. The water remains in the ecology, but it does not solve the proposed receiver without another diversion.

### Pinch-and-release test

Gently pinch or bind one capillary cord for less than a pulse while watching the neighboring branches. The direct receiver dims; water rises elsewhere; releasing the cord restores most flow. This maps redundancy and reveals which branch can tolerate a temporary diversion.

**Weak result:** the binding slips into a root joint and remains after the pulse. The experiment identifies the alternate route but leaves the original branch weak until someone climbs to remove it.

### Soil-acceptance test

Guide equal trickles over pale untreated root, living old soil, and fouled soil. Living ground draws the water inward. Pale root sheds it. Fouled soil produces an oily or mineral film and closes nearby rootlets.

**Weak result:** the fouled trickle reaches a lower receiver before it can be caught. The relation is learned, and a bounded part of that receiver becomes strained until flushed.

## Water for the caravan

The western spare lip can provide every one of the forty-two travelers one full drink during Long Shade if the water is drawn gradually and the spillway remains connected to the lower gutter. Drawn gradually through the connected spillway, this is sustainable overflow. Use the amount as a benchmark; individual skins need not be tracked.

Name the proposal by what happens to the water.

- **Direct drinking at the lip:** sustainable while people arrive in small groups and do not soap, wash wounds, or step into the basin.
- **Filled skins carried west:** sustainable if limited to immediate drinking; the quantity is too small to alter the current materially.
- **A day of water sealed in barrels:** spent from the migration unless equivalent clean water returns before Red Dusk.
- **A canvas trough to camp with a lower return line:** borrowing if both lines remain intact and contamination is controlled.
- **Washing wounds or cloth in the basin:** fouling. Carry water away for use and return only what remains biologically clean.

## Recovery of Moth and Reed

If already recovered, their hoofprints and disturbed cords remain as evidence. If not, both grain mules are on the animal ledge.

- **Moth** stands on the cool western rim and steps with the basin spill. The mule follows Jun Alder, Olan Peat, Tess Coil, or a repeated leaf-click carried from the low hollow.
- **Reed** is not trapped but freezes whenever rain rises past the face. Pulling during a spill causes a backward lunge into the central cords. Waiting for stillness, shielding the eyes, or redirecting a lower trickle allows movement.

Recovering the pair can be a separate operation from mapping the water.

- A calm single-file descent preserves all cords and returns a complete team.
- A hurried drive succeeds but pinches one branch and leaves one or both animals strained.
- Cutting a capillary cord creates immediate space but permanently weakens its receiver unless a bypass is installed.
- Leaving the animals with a broad marked route can be a legitimate informed choice if a handler volunteers and the route's later change is stated.

A recovered team can carry water, pipe, wet soil, lantern supports, or injured people. A loose team may damage the canopy or choose an eastern route beyond the last ordinary exit.

## Diversion families

### Western drinking station

Suspend a small gutter or series of skins beneath the spare lip and return overflow to the lower gutter.

- **Best use:** immediate caravan thirst with minimal ecological strain.
- **Material:** clean containers, line, and attendants who keep washing and animals out.
- **Persistent cost:** little if removed before the basins climb.
- **Later option:** a known western route and hydrated departure groups.

### Copper circulation loop

Use uncut or minimally altered copper to carry water to a camp, soil road, or lantern branch and back to the lower return.

- **Best use:** larger borrowing or a receiver separated by moving roots.
- **Consent:** Ira distinguishes a recoverable loop from crushed, cut, or abandoned pipe.
- **Persistent cost:** a loop left through Root Night may kink into the roots; fouled return water can distribute contamination widely.
- **Later option:** the same loop can cool the soil road's fire margin or restore a dark lantern branch.

### Canvas spillway

Shape clean canvas into a broad temporary course. It carries more water than pipe but loses heat and is vulnerable to root shifts.

- **Best use:** cooling a broad margin, lowering water to the low hollow, or creating a temporary animal-safe crossing.
- **Material:** company canvas or a named private sheet, anchors, and repeated adjustment.
- **Persistent cost:** canvas used near spice oil or blackgrass heat may become unusable or dangerous.
- **Later option:** a lower course can preserve an exit or draw one basin within reach at Root Night.

### Capillary bypass

Bridge a broken or pinched cord with clean flax, hair, reed, cloth, living vine, porous clay, magic, or another medium that wicks water upward.

- **Best use:** one receiver has gone dry while the source remains intact.
- **Material:** small but precise; the line must stay clean and in contact at both ends.
- **Persistent cost:** a bypass usually carries less water than the original cord and may dry if exposed to reflection.
- **Later option:** can raise a weak branch to working or create a redundant path that makes the whole rain current strong.

### Controlled drain

Empty one basin or branch into containers, a firebreak, a rescue pocket, or another urgent use without a return.

- **Best use:** a named immediate danger justifies spending the current.
- **Material:** large carrying capacity and a plan for the newly dry receiver.
- **Persistent cost:** the branch is weak or absent for the remainder of the migration; downstream lantern, memory, cooling, or crown work must adapt.
- **Later option:** saved people, animals, or a preserved route may supply labor or an alternate current. The spent water does not reappear because the use was justified.

## Contamination and repair

### Oil and spice

A sheen causes accepting roots to close. Catch it at the surface with absorbent grain sack, dry clay, charcoal, bark fiber, magic, or another credible method. Remove the fouled material from the current; do not stir it into a larger basin.

### Salt

White Salt makes water look clear while preventing root acceptance. Divert contaminated water away from living receivers. Dilution may protect a branch if the salt load is small, but the salt remains somewhere and should be recorded.

### Blood and medicine

Blood is not automatically poison, but the fungal cords and receiving roots respond unpredictably to it. Water used for treatment should not be returned unless Vey or an equivalent healer can state that it remains clean. Keep treatment water and the living circuit separate unless a healer can certify the return.

### Broken basin

A cracked cradle can be splinted with wood, rope, clay, metal, magic, or root-friendly packing. A sealed bowl without a spill lip is a reservoir, not a restored circuit. Repair must preserve both intake and onward flow.

### Pinched cord

Remove the load, warm the cord, and wait one pulse. If it remains flattened, install a capillary bypass. Cutting away the pinch may restore flow immediately but permanently shortens the branch.

## Stage changes

### Long Shade

The western lip and animal ledge are reachable by ordinary root terraces. Heavy containers, pipe, canvas, and draft animals can move through the region. This is the best stage for mapping and caravan water.

### Red Dusk

The basins climb. The central and eastern branches spill faster. Ordinary routes reach only the lowest bowls. Large diversions must already be installed or moved by rope, flight, climbing, or a working animal route.

### Root Night

Only specialized access reaches the high basins, but a redirected lower course remains usable. Warm rain strongly reveals fungal cords and the Bearer's pulse. A working central branch becomes a route marker toward the crown.

### Glass Dawn

The eastern bowls steam and lose water. Remaining flow is committed to crown and root cooling. New drinking operations or full repairs are no longer practical, though consequences and existing bypasses continue to matter.

## Results and later options

Record each branch and the overall current.

- **Absent:** a needed branch is fully broken, drained, or fouled. Another current cannot replace it.
- **Weak:** reduced or late rain reaches its receivers. Strong soil or light can use that limited flow efficiently, but the current remains a constraint.
- **Working:** western drinking, central ascent, eastern cooling, and lower return remain sufficient for their stated tasks, even if one uses a temporary bypass.
- **Strong:** the circuit has a clean redundant path, restored soil acceptance, and protected cooling capacity. It can absorb one later pinch, keep an early lantern release alive, or cool a strained burn margin.

Show the result through refilling levels, temperature, wet stems, waking cords, cooling roots, and crown mist. The survivors decide what to take, borrow, return, or spend; the circuit displays what follows.

## Late access and moving evidence

When the bowls climb beyond ordinary terraces, marked water, steam, wet cords, and a prepared lower return can still carry basin evidence and function inward. A group may work through a circulation loop or bypass already installed, or through special access actually established in play. It cannot declare an unprepared high-basin repair from the crown.

A clue discovered in marked water or steam remains sourced to the Warm Rain Basins. Drained, salted, oiled, blood-fouled, or broken branches retain their stated consequences when the water reaches another encounter. Receiver wetness alone does not upgrade circulation; the water must cross and return.
