# Blackgrass Burn

**Role:** necessary  
**Tags:** ecology, fire, hazard, moving-region

A low renewal fire must cross the wagon scar, repaired soil corridor, and crownward seam without taking a second road through oil, canvas, pollen, or people.

## Opening View

The blackgrass whitens first. Then a low red line runs the old scar, opening seed shells without smoking—until blue flame leaps sideways into spilled spice. Canvas tightens in the heat. Lantern pollen burns gold above it. On one flank, returned basin water beads on black living soil; on the other, fire reaches the forked wagons. The next gust comes from the Bearer’s vents.

## Encounter Material

## Situation

Blackgrass grows in narrow bands along an old scar across the Bearer's upper roots. Its blades remain matte and cool until heat nears; then they whiten from tip to base, curl away from living margins, and kindle in a low advancing front. Behind it, hard shells open, parasites die, and young roots find a cleared seam.

The front reaches the wagon scar during Red Dusk. There it meets oil, spice dust, loose grain, torn canvas, animal hair, dry fungus, and lantern pollen—each a possible road the old burn never meant to follow. Smother every blade and the crown coats remain sealed. Let every flame run and the repaired soil route, chosen fungal braid, captured dawn, or trapped people may burn with it.

Make the fire legible: fuel, two margins, living-soil breaks, vent wind, intended receiver, and at least two ways out. “Necessary” does not mean “benign,” and a runaway front is never a stronger form of the same current.

## What the fire does

A working heat current performs three related tasks in order:

1. **Renew the migration corridor.** The narrow front burns parasites and dead sheath above living soil without baking the delivered old ground.
2. **Open prepared coats.** Heat reaches crownward shells only after soil, light, rain, and river course have begun to fill them.
3. **Clear flexible root growth.** The old scar guides fire across tissue that can regrow behind it.

Heat too early opens empty shells that collapse. Heat too late leaves complete but sealed knots that travel east. Heat outside the scar consumes dependencies rather than completing them.

Cut blackgrass dries and burns hotter. A carried ember can extend a prepared path; it cannot replace the continuous living corridor and its margins.

## Reading the front

Before intervening, establish five physical facts.

1. **Leading edge:** where blades are whitening but not yet burning.
2. **Left margin:** what living tissue, wet line, old soil, or bare mineral keeps flame from spreading.
3. **Right margin:** the second independent boundary. One sound edge does not make a bounded corridor.
4. **Receiver:** which renewal scar, prepared shell path, or crownward seam the heat is meant to reach.
5. **Escape route:** where people and animals can move if wind, pollen, or reflected heat changes the front.

Then identify exceptional fuels: oil, spice dust, dry canvas, loose grain chaff, cut lantern stems, animal bedding, resinous wood, or spilled lamp wick. State which are inside the corridor, which lie across a margin, and which can be removed only by abandoning useful cargo.

A clear inspection reveals the front's behavior. Use a system check for time, a concealed oil run, or reaching a threatened load without exposure.

## The three burn segments

The fire crosses three distinct segments. Work in one may help another; resolve each segment on its own fuel, margins, and receiver.

### Wagon scar

The first segment runs through the place where the upper wagon crushed the soilbearer road. Spilled oil follows one root seam; spice dust lies across another. The fire must cross enough of the old scar to renew it without following either contaminant into the restored road or wagon pocket.

- **Primary problem:** exceptional fuel and trapped salvage.
- **Useful boundaries:** dark living soil, damp root flesh, cleared mineral fittings, and a returned basin gutter.
- **Common choice:** preserve intact wagon material or dismantle it to remove fuel and open a safe working lane.
- **Later effect:** a sound crossing leaves the repaired soil route biologically active and heat-ready instead of scorched.

### Restored corridor

The second segment travels beside or through whatever repair the party built for the soilbearers. The correct method depends on that repair. Canvas troughs need wet backing. Copper scent-rills conduct heat and must be isolated from dry packing. Hand-ferry routes require crews to clear before ignition. A shaded detour may need a carried ember rather than the original front.

- **Primary problem:** deliver heat after living soil has passed, not through the beetle stream.
- **Useful boundaries:** old-ground ribbons, wet capillary lines, lifted bridge pieces, and already-charred scar tissue.
- **Common choice:** pause soil delivery briefly, split labor between animal control and fire control, or risk overlap to preserve time.
- **Later effect:** a working corridor can carry both soil and bounded heat in sequence, creating several heat-ready shells.

### Crownward seam

The final segment approaches a root gap that opens only on the Bearer's deep exhale. Lantern pollen may already cross above it, and the selected fungal course may pass nearby in wet cords.

- **Primary problem:** synchronize the front with root-breath while protecting light, rain, and memory paths.
- **Useful boundaries:** cooled root lips, wet pollen stems, cleared crownward runners, and an ember held below the seam.
- **Common choice:** delay the main front, carry a small prepared ember, or accept a weak late heat current rather than risk an early jump.
- **Later effect:** prepared coats open during the safe interval instead of charring or remaining sealed.

## Preparing a bounded corridor

A defensible corridor has four elements.

- **Continuous intended fuel:** living blackgrass or a short prepared ember path that reaches the next living band.
- **Two independent margins:** wet, mineral, living-soil, or already-charred boundaries on both sides.
- **A prepared receiver:** renewed scar or filled shell path, not merely somewhere the party wants fire to go.
- **A maintained escape:** at least one route that does not cross the projected front or close on the same root pulse.

Available methods include:

### Clear lateral fuel

Move canvas, chaff, bedding, spice dust, cut stems, rope fibers, and loose wood away from both margins.

- **Requirements:** labor, time, and authority over salvage that may be damaged or abandoned.
- **Visible result:** the grass front remains low instead of throwing sparks into adjacent material.
- **Persistent cost:** useful cargo may be scattered, soaked, dismantled, or left behind.
- **Later option:** one margin can survive without continuous watering.

### Lay a returned wet line

Divert working basin water along a margin and return the unused flow through a lower gutter.

- **Requirements:** an intact source, path, and return; a watcher at the downstream receiver.
- **Visible result:** steam rises in a narrow line while root tissue behind it remains cool and flexible.
A dead-end splash looks equivalent for a few breaths but steams dry; only a line with an intact return stays cool through the passing front.
- **Persistent cost:** the water branch is committed during the burn and cannot simultaneously support a large caravan diversion.
- **Later option:** the corridor can pass near fungal cords or lantern stems without automatically destroying them.

### Build a living-soil guide

Use delivered old ground as a dark, moist ribbon at the edge of the intended front. Blackgrass bends toward the renewal scar and stops at healthy pellets.

- **Requirements:** working soil delivery and protection from oil, sterile salt, and crushing traffic.
- **Visible result:** heat crosses above the guide while beetle pellets remain dark and biologically active.
- **Persistent cost:** the soil route cannot carry heavy human loads during the crossing.
- **Later option:** the same corridor can deliver soil first and heat second to several crown shells.

### Make a mineral break

Clear down to nonflammable shell, stone, wet clay, or clean metal fittings. White Salt alone is not a safe break near the Glass Waste; reflective crystals can intensify heat.

- **Requirements:** tools and a route for removed hot material.
- **Visible result:** the flame shortens and turns inward at the cleared strip.
- **Persistent cost:** the break may sever shallow rootlets or close a soilbearer shortcut.
- **Later option:** a small crew can hold a threatened cargo edge without basin water.

### Hold and ferry an ember

Separate a small burning clump in a lidded shovel, wet clay cradle, stone tray, or clean iron basket, then carry it across a temporarily closed gap to prepared living blackgrass.

- **Requirements:** a receiving band, heat protection, and a route cleared of pollen and dry dust.
- **Visible result:** the main front can be delayed while the later segment remains possible.
- **Persistent cost:** the carried ember weakens quickly and becomes dangerous if fed cut grass or oil.
- **Later option:** the party can wait for root-breath rather than forcing the entire front against a closed seam.

### Delay one segment

Strip a short section of blackgrass with roots intact, cool it below ignition, or lift it away from friction until the receiver is ready.

- **Requirements:** careful handling and a plan to reconnect the heat path.
- **Visible result:** the front stops at a clean gap instead of scattering.
- **Persistent cost:** delay consumes Root Night access and may produce a weak late current.
- **Later option:** soil, light, rain, or memory can arrive before heat.

## Running the burn

Use this sequence once ignition is chosen.

1. **Name the segment and receiver.** “Protect the wagons” is not enough; state where the front should end.
2. **Show both margins.** Identify the people, water, material, or living system holding each one.
3. **Move nonparticipants and animals.** A controlled operation still needs a fallback route.
4. **Ignite or admit the front.** Use existing blackgrass whenever possible. Added accelerant creates an uncontrolled fuel path.
5. **Check source, margins, receiver, and neighbor.** Show what burned, what stayed cool, whether the intended receiver changed, and what adjacent current was strained.
6. **Record the scar.** A successful segment leaves a visible line of blackened sheath with flexible damp tissue and living soil behind it.

Let failure follow the method: a wet line runs dry, a mineral break reflects heat, an animal crosses a margin, an ember fades before the seam opens, or oil starts a second front. Never substitute a generic loss of control for a consequence already present in the fiction.

## Current and hazard states

Record **bounded blackgrass heat** separately from any **runaway fire**.

- **Absent heat current:** the intended corridor is fully smothered, severed, diverted away from prepared receivers, or missed after the crown hardens.
- **Weak heat current:** intermittent or late heat reaches part of the prepared path; one filled shell may open, or the corridor may require a costly second ember.
- **Working heat current:** a low continuous front reaches the prepared renewal scar and crownward receiver after the other currents arrive.
- **Strong heat current:** both margins are redundant, a held ember or second living branch exists, and the working front can absorb one later gap, dry patch, or shortened breath interval.

A runaway fire is not a strong current. Track it as a separate front with its own fuel, margins, and threatened receivers. Crews may abandon the intended heat current to contain it, split their work, or accept damage in one region to preserve another.

## People and property in the burn

**Pelen Marr** knows the wagon fittings and accepts responsibility for the crushed road. Pelen will dismantle damaged company wood and personal shares with consent, but will not claim that guilt gives him title to everyone else's cargo.

**Mara Doss**, **Oren Pike**, and the teamsters can move animals before ignition or use trained teams to drag wet lines and heavy breaks. Animals left near whitening grass become a route problem before they become casualties.

**Ira Senn** owns the longest intact copper. Ira may lend it for a returned wet line if the pipe remains recoverable or if the party states why losing it is necessary. Heating sealed or oil-tainted copper can burst it.

**Corven Ash** owns the spice load whose dust can carry flame. Corven will sacrifice contaminated bags once shown the front. They resist destroying sealed stock merely because spice is present somewhere nearby.

**Nahl Reed** insists that burning the only viable seed is not justified by calling fire natural. Nahl can recognize a prepared shell but cannot command the operation.

## Complications and failure-forward consequences

- Oil ignites along a shallow seam. Isolate the second front, abandon nearby salvage, or divert the main heat current away from the road.
- A wet margin protects the fungal braid but drains faster than expected. The course survives while the heat current becomes weak, or vice versa.
- Lantern pollen arrives above the corridor before the crew clears cut stems. Close one light path, delay ignition, or risk a crownward jump.
- A draft animal crosses a cleared break. Recovering it may scatter fuel, consume the escape route, or require the teamsters to abandon another operation.
- A successful wagon-scar crossing reaches a closed crown seam. An ember hold, delayed segment, or the Bearer's preliminary breath is needed to test the gap.
- The front is fully smothered to save people. The adventure continues with an absent heat current: prepared knots remain sealed unless the party can establish a later ember route.

Preserving life may close the seed route. Record the changed state without grading the choice.

## Stage changes

- **Long Shade:** the distant smoke and old scar can be studied; fuel can be moved before the front arrives.
- **Red Dusk:** the fire crosses the wagon scar and restored road. Heavy breaks and salvage decisions must already be in place.
- **Root Night:** a bounded front or held ember can pass the crownward seam on the deep exhale.
- **Glass Dawn:** reflected light creates a second ignition risk. A late operation becomes containment and departure rather than careful preparation.

## Results and later options

After the burn, record:

- the state of each segment: wagon scar, restored corridor, and crownward seam;
- the source and both margins used;
- exceptional fuel removed, spent, or left in place;
- whether the soil route remained biologically active;
- whether lantern, rain, or memory paths were strained;
- whether a runaway front exists and what it threatens;
- whether the bounded heat current is absent, weak, working, or strong;
- whether an ember remains available for the deep breath; and
- which people, animals, cargo, and exits remain on each side of the scar.

Leave the scar with an exact account of what the fire opened, what it consumed, and what it still threatens.

## Late access and moving evidence

The fire front is itself a moving carrier. Opened shells, whitening margins, smoke behavior, and a held ember can preserve burn evidence after the party leaves the old scar. Record the clue under the Blackgrass Burn and keep the intended front separate from any runaway fire.

Avoiding the burn may knowingly leave the crown coat sealed. A late crew may ferry a held ember only along a crownward seam already prepared with fuel continuity, two margins, a receiver, and an escape route. Once Glass Dawn reflection creates an uncontrolled second ignition, excess flame cannot be relabeled as bounded heat, and a missed crownward seam cannot be improvised after the coat hardens.
