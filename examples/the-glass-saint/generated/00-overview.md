## Synopsis

A museum reliquary bursts on the eve of Saint Olyra’s procession. Before sunset, independent witnesses must recover its stolen petition-bed, expose the lie beneath the city’s obedient saint, and decide whether grief may borrow a body and command through every bell.

## Premise

At noon on the eve of Saint Olyra’s procession, the rehearsal bell bursts the museum reliquary from within. The painted devotional copy remains; the engraved silver bed beneath it has been cut away. Curator Iria Vale invokes the emergency witness charter, opening the sealed gallery, restricted archive, procession court, and abandoned bell chapel to independent inspection. Her authority ends at Vale Manor, where her family keeps Olyra’s true heartglass, and her interest prevents her from certifying the case against them. The council will not halt the sunset procession without proof of immediate danger. Witnesses, records, petitioners, bellkeepers, and the divided Vales can establish what Tavia Sorn stole, what Olyra died doing, and what Edrin Vale has built to make the city hear. A warrant, a public case, a broken peal, a bargain, or forced entry may reach the winter garden. None decides who should hold the relic or what the recovered truth requires.

## Explanation

Sixty-three years ago, museum founder and former council archivist Alis Vale replaced Saint Olyra’s heartglass with a painted devotional copy. During the Ash Fever, Olyra defied the council, opened the sealed west infirmary, and died among the patients she refused to abandon. The civic cult kept her authority by reversing her act: Olyra became the saint of obedience. Alis hid her deposition and the names of the patrons who ordered the doors sealed, but kept the authentic heartglass at Vale Manor. The trustees found the substitution during recent conservation and chose silence again.

Alis’s grandson Edrin has spent years authenticating the record. When the trustees offered one more closed review after the procession, he chose the hour when the city would already be listening. Assistant conservator Tavia Sorn joined him because the withheld material included her family’s petitions and an ancestor’s infirmary record. She set a bell-metal answer-pin beneath the case. The rehearsal note made it ring, threw the glass outward, and hid her removal of the silver vow-bed. Tavia carried the bed through the procession court, passed it to Edrin, and fled with a cut archive leaf toward the House of Petitions. She expects the rite to give the buried record a voice. She does not know that it takes one.

The rite joins four materials: Olyra’s heartglass, the petition-engraved vow-bed, knot geometry rehearsed in the Bell Chapel, and panes from Olyra’s first shrine. Six decades of pleas, thanks, slogans, and demands give the silver its language. The heartglass contributes a residue of Olyra, never a recoverable person. The procession peal gives the result reach. The first long note raises a body from reflected petitions. The second takes the natural voice of the nearest living Vale inside the winter garden’s outer pane circle. The third sends one command through every fitted procession bell until dawn. Edrin begins on the speaker mark and intends to pay the vocal cost. Iria is endangered only if she enters the circle or remains there after Edrin is moved beyond it. The mouth stage fails without either a Vale inside or a hand closing the inner line.

Six answer-pins cast from the old infirmary bell connect the apparatus. The museum trigger is cracked and spent. Three pins in the Grand Belfry carry the witness, mouth, and crown notes. Relay pins at East Gate and River Ward repeat the crown command but cannot raise the body or take a speaker. The chapel holds a working counterkey: a notched tuning strip that identifies and lifts live pins, a braided reversal cord that changes the outer knot, and a pitch table keyed to Olyra’s deposition. Each part enables different work. At the belfry, investigators can prevent a stage, confine the crown note, sound a warning, or preserve the peal; every cut rope, lifted pin, and altered knot remains altered.

The investigation contests who may make a fact public. Provost Helian Dorr and the trustees fear riot, scandal, and institutional ruin. They will preserve the procession until confronted with evidence they cannot fold back into a private review. The House of Petitions claims the vow-bed as public property and can provide witnesses, custodians, or current petitions. Bellmaster Jori Kest can delay, mute, or retune the peal when given technical proof and either lawful or public protection. Edrin will pause for a hearing that already possesses records, witnesses, and a custody plan. He will not surrender to another sealed promise. The finale may preserve or destroy the heartglass, dismantle the speaking body, permit a bounded answering rite, or allow the full awakening. Whatever speaks is a composite of relic, record, petition, and civic expectation: a disputed civic voice assembled by the living.

Advance pressure through acts the table can see. Dorr issues containment after a report, accusation, or request reaches him. The answer-pins wake after Edrin has assembled the manor apparatus and Dorr has authorized the final bell test. The procession moves when its hold expires or fails. The first note needs the grand-tower crew; the second needs Edrin to close the inner knot around a Vale; the third needs the crown bell and an intact launch pin. Change any of those facts and the sequence changes. Edrin and Dorr learn through runners, watch reports, public bells, and witnesses. Neither answers information that never reaches him.

Carry five public facts from scene to scene: how widely the rupture’s cause is known; where authenticated copies are held; what authority Captain Renn possesses; whether the procession follows its published course; and which witnesses have committed to speak. These facts persist. A public copy cannot be recalled, a signed witness keeps the commitment, and a written denial becomes evidence of its own. Gains may conflict: publicity can protect records and frighten families; a warrant can secure objects and concentrate them under one seal. Carry those mixed results into the belfry and manor.

Keep four pages distinct during route-open play. **Access** names the doors, routes, and scheduled spaces that can be reached. **Knowledge** names supported claims. **Authority** names what a charter, seal, warrant, custodian, or witness permits. **Ritual state** names the pins, knots, materials, notes, and relays still in play. Knowledge of a place grants no entry; a warrant proves no history; a maintenance passage supplies no counterkey; a stopped bell settles no custody. When separated investigators reunite, record what each saw, changed, received permission for, or still only infers.

Unvisited places continue through fixed hands and channels. Tavia goes from the court to the House unless intercepted. Dorr acts on reports that reach him. Senna logs and protects the archive; publication requires someone present to choose and carry a packet. Petition caucuses carry their own copies and demands. The chapel counterkey stays where Edrin left it until someone removes it. The belfry crew follows the latest order and evidence placed before them. Record each off-screen movement. Late arrival changes custody, access, and pressure; the location and its people retain what has already happened.

Before the winter-garden doors open, carry forward the facts already made. Record the surviving stage and relay pins, any warning peal, the holders of the records, Renn’s authority, the witnesses in motion, and the positions of Iria and Tavia. Six ways into the winter garden remain: a warranted carriage gate, Iria’s family claim, Tavia’s service route, the infirmary-frame maintenance passage, a public advance behind the warning crowd, or direct force. Each brings different witnesses, hazards, and claims to custody.

The winter garden binds five things the rite cannot lose: the shrine-pane crown, the vow-bed, the heartglass cradle, the inner mouth knot, and the bell trunk under the floor. The mouth note takes the nearest living Vale inside the outer pane circle when the note completes. Edrin begins nearest. Iria becomes the target only by entering or remaining inside after he leaves. The ordinary transfer fails without a Vale inside the circle or a hand closing the inner line. The complete counterkey can instead bind a willing breath-giver into a limited answering form. That speaker risks muteness until dawn rather than involuntary permanent loss.

First decide what the bells did to a body and voice. Then decide what the city does with it. Record whether the body formed, whose voice was taken, the command and its range, the surviving condition and custodian of every object, the witnesses to each stage, and all injuries or detentions. Then record publication, prosecution, surviving compulsion, and Olyra’s changed public memory. Edrin’s surrender and silent bells leave the public argument intact. The city may recover the truth of Olyra’s defiance and still disagree about ownership, punishment, sainthood, and what defiance demands now.
