# Full Playthrough: The Lantern Measure in Bramblewick

This playthrough follows one complete road through *The Last Bell of Bramblewick*. It is not a prescribed order. The checked-in journal preserves the same visits, discovered clues, established conclusions, correction, and consequences.

The route is:

```text
Hall -> Orchard -> Burrow -> Chest -> Chapel -> School
     -> Kettle -> Hedge -> Hall -> Moot
```

The company visits eight of the ten investigative locations, returns once to Hearth Hall, and spots eighty-four of the adventure's 123 clues. The journal records no missed-clue event. Of the thirty-nine unseen clues, nineteen belong to the unvisited Moss Apothecary and Bramble Mill; twenty are alternative discoveries in encounters the company did visit.

## The investigators

The Lantern Measure consists of Teren Malk, a former circuit bailiff; Pella Reedbank, a halfling advocate familiar with hearth records; Sable Quoin, a dwarven book examiner; and Fen Harrow, a route surveyor. Merrit Alder invited them as neutral witnesses to his morning audit. They expected a procedural quarrel and arrived without any mandate to punish.

By the time the Founders' Supper goes silent, Merrit's signed invitation has already made them outside audit witnesses. Bramblewick's storm custom then divides temporary authority: Constable Wil Sloe controls the scene and custody; civic custodians control their records; Hester keeps order but cannot judge her own accounts; and the Lantern Measure may preserve common property, inspect civic records with their custodians, question willing witnesses, and present a recommendation at first bell.

## Session One: Nine on the clock

### The body in the map room

Bram Alder comes into the supper hall wet at the shoulders and white around the mouth.

“Merrit's down,” he says. “The clock came with him.”

Everyone moves at once. Hester shouts for the hall door to be closed. Perrin Moss pushes toward the map room. Orlo Vane catches two pupils who have begun to follow. Mara Kettle tells someone to mind the pantry fire and then forgets who she told.

Elian reaches the map-room threshold first and stops the others there. He does not announce that the room is a crime scene. He asks Wil to preserve the threshold under the storm custom and asks Hester to acknowledge Merrit's outside-witness commission. Wil closes the public hall door; Hester agrees before Bram can object.

Merrit lies beneath the tallcase clock with his left shoulder under its lower case. The face reads nine exactly. Blood has crossed the north edge of the rug. Fine rain comes through the open window and stipples the blood nearest the sill.

Perrin begins, “His heart—”

“No,” Fen says, kneeling without touching the body. “Not yet.”

The Lantern Measure separates the visible facts before hearing explanations. The wound behind Merrit's left ear is compact, not broad. Some clock glass lies on bare floor, but several shards rest on blood. The pendulum is not broken. A stub of blue chalk has been forced between it and the case.

Sable asks the most useful dull question of the night: “Who has keys?”

The cabinet register names Merrit, Hester, Bram for household and orchard business, and Orlo as an authorized copyist. The screened clerk's-passage lock is undamaged; the passage joins the covered service way behind the long pantry and reaches the map room without crossing the public floor. The north garden door is pulled closed but unlatched.

Pella inventories Merrit's pockets. A folded note says that the old folios at home will speak if he cannot. His audit folio lies open to three hearth entries marked with crossed-out chimneys. Marginal references point to the Common Chest, the Chapel of the Open Door, and school warrants. The pages that would name the responsible copyist have been torn from the stitching.

Elian asks that the clock glass, map weights, medicine bottle, audit folio, and desk cloth be kept under separate coverings. He does not yet search the acorn-shaped weights. The company has enough facts to proceed and too many hands in the hall.

### Six first statements

The suspects are questioned separately in the long room while villagers gather in the rain outside.

Orlo says he led the school rehearsal through the last bell. He admits owning the copyist's key and says Merrit expected to speak with him after supper, but that he never went to the map room. His first concern is whether the pupils will be questioned.

Bram says he argued with his uncle and went directly to the orchard. He denies having entered the map room before finding the body. He watches the desk whenever Pella mentions Merrit's papers.

Mara denies poisoning Merrit before anyone has accused her of poison. She denies making private payments to him and asks whether the pantry ledger is really part of the inquiry.

Cora Bramble says flour travels on half the village and conceals the scrape on Merrit's wrist beneath a general complaint about his lending practices.

Perrin calls the death a seizure followed by a fall. When Fen asks how a fall made the focused wound, Perrin retreats to uncertainty and asks to take the medicine bottle back to his shop.

Hester acknowledges Merrit's prior commission and Wil's scene authority, then asks that the Common Chest remain sealed until its second keyholder and a neutral witness can be assembled. The request is procedurally proper and personally convenient.

Bram becomes the company's first working suspect. He had motive, had argued with Merrit, found the body, and lied about entering a room that holds his pipe ash and a thread consistent with his cuff. Elian writes the theory as a question rather than a conclusion:

> Did Bram return from the orchard, kill Merrit over the will, and stage the clock to exploit the bell?

Fen asks to test the orchard before the storm destroys it. Elian would rather search Merrit's burrow first. The company chooses the perishable evidence.

Before leaving, they record all of the obvious routes. Merrit's burrow may contain the working papers. The Copper Kettle can reconstruct service and movement. Moss Apothecary can test the medicine. Bramble Mill can compare flour and rain. Alder Orchard can test Bram. The Common Chest holds the warrants. The chapel holds the deaths. The school holds Orlo's alibi. The North Hedge holds the departure route. Hester has already summoned the First-Bell Moot.

The murder is not yet solved, but it cannot be made linear by one blocked door.

### Alder Orchard

Rain runs down the orchard terraces. Bram's dog, Nutmeg, is still tethered beside a storm-damaged pear tree. A brace cable has pulled low across the path. Fresh cuts show where Bram freed it before it tore down two younger trees.

Fen works from sequence rather than shape. Rain lies on the exposed cuts but has not penetrated the sawdust compressed beneath the cable. Nutmeg's chain has wrapped twice around the post as Bram moved between the tree and tool barrow. Bram's boots circle the work site. His pruning knife lies where it fell in wet grass.

Bram could have left the orchard. The evidence does not create a magical boundary around him. But a round trip to the hall, a killing, a staged clock, and a return before the rain would require the cuts, dog, and weather to have happened in an order they did not.

Pella then asks Bram to roll back his right cuff. A green-brown thread is missing. The tobacco in his pipe matches the sweetgrass ash near Merrit's desk.

Bram curses his uncle, the four outsiders, and finally himself.

“I went in before supper,” he says. “Six, perhaps a little after. I wanted the will. He'd written another one. He always wrote another one when he wanted me frightened.”

He admits that Merrit caught him at the desk and threatened to read a disinheritance clause at the next moot. He left angry and lied because he expected every trace in the room to be treated as murder evidence.

Elian makes the mistake the journal preserves. After matching the cuff, he adds a note stating that the torn cloth proves Bram returned during the murder interval. Fen objects immediately: the cloth establishes presence, not time, and Bram has just supplied a testable earlier event. The orchard chronology contradicts the return.

The company voids the overstatement rather than erasing it. The corrected note says:

> Bram lied about an earlier desk search, but the storm work independently accounts for the murder interval. His guilt is trespass and attempted will-hunting, not Merrit's death.

The correction matters to Elian. A case that cannot show where it changed its mind is not stronger for appearing never to have been wrong.

At the low gate, Fen finds shallow rounded impressions made before the mud formed. A dark blue thread catches on split hazel. The track does not run from the orchard work site; it uses the old North Hedge line toward the school. From the upper terrace, the open north window of Hearth Hall is visible. Rain struck it before anyone found Merrit.

Bram is no longer the best murder theory. He is still not a trustworthy witness.

## Session Two: Two kinds of theft

### Merrit Alder's burrow

Merrit's burrow is warm, dry, and arranged like a mind that expected to outlive every quarrel. The public room contains respectable account folios. The archive room contains leverage.

Pella refuses to let the company begin by opening every sealed packet. They first reconstruct Merrit's audit table. Three hearths have ages that cannot be reconciled with births, marriages, or adult registration. Burial dates appear beside the names. School initials recur beside the disbursements. A working sheet cites exact Common Chest warrant numbers.

A recent clockwright's receipt says the Hearth Hall clock had been serviced and kept time within a minute. Merrit's inventory sketch records a crescent casting flaw in one of the brass acorn weights. The sketch will matter later, but it does not yet prove that the weight struck him.

The drawer thread and will drafts corroborate Bram's earlier search. Merrit's index decodes Mara's regular envelopes under the phrase “Kettle smoke.” Older treatment cards suggest Perrin had once prescribed more medicine than his current ledger may admit. A sealed ergot memorandum names Cora. A separate bridge file names Hester.

The archive creates a temptation: every suspect has a secret, therefore any secret may be the motive. Pella states the opposite rule.

“Merrit kept them separate because separate shame gave him separate leverage. We should not make his filing cabinet into one conspiracy because murder makes us impatient.”

Sable nevertheless sees Hester's marks on disputed payments and gives the reeve more weight than the other suspects. Hester controls procedure, controls access to the chest, delayed opening it, and has a financial discrepancy large enough to matter. Sable's working theory is that Hester used fictitious hearths to conceal the bridge money and killed Merrit before he named her.

The archive also strengthens the physical case. The clock was sound before supper. It was stopped with chalk and broken after blood spread. Nine was chosen to borrow the school rehearsal's public sound.

Orlo's access begins to matter. Merrit's note records that Orlo requested the old burial register several years before the false hearths first appeared. That fact does not yet prove fraud. It connects the schoolmaster to the source from which the names could have been selected.

The company takes only the working audit, the clock receipt, the acorn sketch, the relevant index pages, and the draft moot agenda. The other leverage files remain sealed.

### The Common Chest

The Common Chest sits behind two oak doors in the reeve's office. Hester assembles Sister Amity and Nim Thatch, the retired mill clerk, as witnesses. Sable approves the witnesses and distrusts Hester's eagerness to be seen approving them.

The bridge file is opened first.

Hester admits that the east pier shifted before the autumn rains. The moot had postponed repairs twice because no family wanted the assessment. She ordered stone and labor without a vote, then spread the payments across discretionary lines so the total would not trigger an emergency hearing.

Sable expects a gap. Instead, every payment ends in delivered stone, iron cramps, cart hire, or named labor. The amount is exact. The offense is not invented work or private gain. Hester used common money without authority and concealed the act afterward.

The ghost-hearth warrants are different. The first forms retain small memorial crosses beside the names. Later copies omit them. For six years, grain and coin allotted to Dolly Reed, Milo Tansy, and Pipkin Wold pass through clean-copy authorizations in Orlo's hand. The redemptions end in school meal, shoe, slate, oil, and winter-cloth accounts.

Sable abandons her Hester theory because the documents require it, not because Hester becomes sympathetic. She prepares two separate findings:

- Hester committed an unauthorized public expenditure and concealed it.
- Someone created fictitious hearths from memorial names and routed their shares through Orlo's school accounts.

Pella asks whether the beneficiary names are necessary. Sable initially says that a complete account requires a final recipient. Pella replies that the murder case requires proof of the diversion, Orlo's control, Merrit's threatened disclosure, and the resulting motive. It does not require naming which children wore the shoes.

They agree on a sealed process. Three custodians may verify that the beneficiary box corresponds to the school redemptions without reading the names into the moot record. Sable copies the warrant numbers, handwriting changes, receiving marks, and totals. Nim can certify the chain.

The company now has a motive that belongs particularly to Orlo. Merrit's announcement threatened both the forged civic records and the private families Orlo believed he was protecting. Hester's offense remains serious but no longer explains the ghost hearths.

### Chapel of the Open Door

The chapel register is kept beneath a carved lintel depicting a door without hinges. Sister Amity does not object to the inquiry. She objects to a room full of villagers learning why the Lantern Measure has come.

The three names are found quickly.

Dolly Reed died at six. Milo Tansy died at nine. Pipkin Wold died before his fifth birthday. Annual notices remove each child from the living household roll. There is no later birth, adoption, marriage, return, or succession that could create an adult hearth under the same name.

Orlo borrowed the register before the first fictitious entry appeared. He said he was preparing a memorial copying exercise for the school. The preserved program uses the same three names. The selection is not random and is not Hester's work.

Sister Amity offers certified copies that prove the deaths and register loan without exposing who later received school aid. Elian asks whether she will testify that Orlo created the false hearths.

“No,” she says. “I will testify that the children died, that no adult household succeeded them, and that Orlo borrowed the book. You have other books for what he wrote afterward.”

Elian accepts the distinction.

The company can now state that three dead children's names became fictitious adult households. It can also state that Orlo selected those names before the false entries and controlled the fund they fed. It still needs to break the public alibi and place him on the route.

## Session Three: The last stanza

### Bramblewick School

The schoolhouse is one long room with low benches, a copyist's cabinet, a stove, and colored chalk kept in shallow wooden trays. Parents have gathered outside. Some demand that the children be sent home. Others demand that Orlo be allowed to remain with them.

Pella refuses to ask a child, “Did your teacher kill Merrit?” Pella and Elian separate the pupils and asks each to describe the rehearsal: which verses they sang, who turned the practice sheets, when the candle was trimmed, which door opened, and where Orlo stood.

Four stanzas were completed without Orlo. The older pupils began because an undelivered supper note told them not to wait. Jory Pike saw Orlo enter through the side yard at about 8:54. A neighbor heard only Orlo's voice in the final stanza, the one that ended at the last bell.

The documents support the children. Practice sheets show four completed chalk marks in different hands. The candle band burned through the same interval. Nibs's rain notation appears before Orlo's correction in the rehearsal margin.

The alibi does not disappear. It becomes narrower. Orlo was publicly present at nine. The murder happened before nine, and the rehearsal proves only that he arrived in time to be heard at the bell.

The school also supplies the staging materials. A blue chalk stick has been snapped from the tray. Its fracture joins the stub wedged in the clock. Orlo's copyist key carries white lime from the screened map-room jamb beneath later mud. The school wash basin contains diluted blood and a green collar fiber. Orlo's blue cuff has been washed recently. Nothing legible remains of the two folio leaves: Orlo fed them through the banked stove's rear ash door before Jory saw him enter, but the same handwriting and redemption chain survives across the other records.

The beneficiary box is indexed by the same ghost-hearth warrant numbers. Pella, Sable, and Sister Amity inspect the index and totals while leaving the recipient leaves sealed. The fund is real. It paid for real needs. It was also created through forgery and controlled by one person without review.

Orlo changes his account twice. First he says he entered through the front door before the fourth stanza. When told that several pupils saw the front bench empty, he says he may have used the side door but arrived before the second stanza. He explains the washed cuff as rainwater and the key lime as ordinary civic dust.

Elian wants to detain him immediately. Fen says the evidence justifies detention but that the route should still be walked before the public case. Pella wants the supper chronology reconstructed without forcing the children to carry more than they have already said.

Hester places Orlo under Wil Sloe's watch. He is not yet publicly accused.

### The Copper Kettle and Long Pantry

The Copper Kettle has spent the night feeding the villagers who are waiting to hear whom the outsiders will name. Mara meets the Lantern Measure in the long pantry with the expression of someone who has decided which secret she would rather confess.

Elsie Sedge carried the school tray across the green when the first rain began. She remembers because the drops spotted the pie cloth and because the last bell had not yet rung. Her route places rain at about 8:50 independently of the body and orchard.

The pantry staff saw Orlo behind the service passage before rehearsal. He asked whether the school tray had gone, then left by the school side. No witness claims to have watched him continuously. Their account establishes departure before the children began and does not by itself say where he went.

During the murder interval, a cider crock burst in the pantry. Mara, Hester, Elsie, and another server spent several minutes saving food and sweeping pottery. Hester served the last pie after Merrit's announcement. The same event covers both women while leaving each of their concealed offenses intact.

Mara produces a burned demand from the kitchen stove. Merrit knew about untaxed plum brandy stored in an old drainage tunnel. Her envelopes paid him to remain silent. She took the cellar ledger during the confusion because she expected the murder inquiry to expose the trade.

Elian asks whether Merrit was drugged through the wine.

Mara says Perrin measured one spoon of tincture in front of the staff. More importantly, Merrit walked steadily afterward, argued coherently, and carried the audit folio to the map room. The focused wound remains the cause requiring explanation.

The school meal ledger repeats the same initials and warrant numbers found in the Common Chest. The covered service passage joins the North Hedge line. Orlo's undelivered rehearsal note told the pupils to begin without him.

By the time they leave the pantry, the company can state three independent timing claims:

- Merrit bled before rain reached the open window.
- Rain began before the last bell.
- Orlo reached the school only after four stanzas had been sung.

Mara is cleared of murder and exposed for brandy. Hester is cleared of murder and exposed for unauthorized spending. Neither result excuses the underlying conduct.

Cora and Perrin remain unresolved. The Lantern Measure has leads to the mill and apothecary, but those inquiries are no longer necessary to identify the killer. Elian asks whether leaving them open will make the public case look incomplete.

Pella answers, “Only if we pretend the charge is that Bramblewick contains one guilty person.”

### The North Hedge

The old lane begins behind Hearth Hall's garden wall, crosses a low hazel gate, skirts the orchard's lower terrace, and ends at the school wash yard. In dry weather it is a quick route. In the present mud it looks slower than it was.

Fen reconstructs the sequence from protected ground. The first crossing rounded under rain before the later searchers cut sharp prints into the mud. The garden door can be pulled closed by its strap from outside but cannot be latched after departure. White lime lies beneath darker mud in one impression. A dark blue cuff thread remains at the gate.

Fen walks the dry-distance line at a halfling's urgent pace: five minutes and forty seconds from the map-room door to the school side yard. The interval fits departure around 8:48 and arrival around 8:54.

Elian asks whether the track is Orlo's.

“No,” Fen says. “It is a small boot on a village path. The route is the route. Orlo becomes the traveler because he had the key, carried the lime, lost the cloth, arrived wet at the side door, washed at the basin, and lied about the time.”

That answer shapes the final case. The company will not invite the moot to convict from a footprint. It will show that no other suspect joins all of the independent facts.

The hedge also finishes the accusation threshold. The Common Chest supplies the motive exhibit. The school joins the false alibi, chalk, key, basin, and beneficiary chain. The route demonstrates opportunity without pretending unique track identification.

Orlo can now be accused coherently without a confession and without visiting the mill or apothecary.

## Session Four: First bell

### Return to Hearth Hall

Before dawn, the company returns to the map room. The coverings remain in place. Wil Sloe has kept everyone out except Hester and a second witness who relieved him.

Fen and Elian inspect the brass map weights. One acorn has a crescent nick matching Merrit's inventory sketch. Its broad surfaces have been wiped. Dark residue and a green collar fiber remain in the engraved seam.

The company identifies the likely weapon. It does not rebuild the case around it. Without a healer's exact comparison from Moss Apothecary, the weight is strong corroboration rather than a singular forensic certainty. The proof of guilt still rests on the time, staging, access, route, alibi, motive, and convergence.

The Lantern Measure prepares four sets of material.

Sable assembles a narrow account exhibit: the dead-child names, warrant sequence, Orlo's authorizations, and school redemptions. Beneficiary names remain sealed.

Fen prepares the door strap, route measurement, weather sequence, and a diagram distinguishing the pre-rain crossing from later search tracks.

Pella prepares separated witness statements. No child is asked to stand alone before Orlo. Sister Amity certifies the deaths and register loan. Nim certifies the Common Chest copies. Mara's and Hester's collateral admissions are reduced to what is needed to explain their suspicious evidence.

Elian writes six propositions on separate cards. If one fails, the moot can identify the exact gap rather than vote on whether the company seems convincing.

The company also divides Merrit's archive. Murder exhibits remain under seal. Valid civic records return to their offices. Unrelated leverage files are not read aloud merely because death has made them accessible.

### The First-Bell Moot

Bramblewick's moot fills Hearth Hall before sunrise. Halflings stand on benches and window ledges. The school pupils sit together near the south hearth with Sister Amity. Orlo sits beside Wil Sloe, not bound, wearing a dry blue coat over the shirt whose cuff has been washed.

Hester opens the hearing by stating Merrit's commission and its limits. The Lantern Measure may present facts and a conclusion. The moot may detain and make a village finding. Final punishment belongs to the circuit magistrate.

Elian begins with the time of death.

Merrit suffered a focused fatal blow. Blood spread before rain came through the open window. Elsie crossed the green in the first rain before the bell. Bram saw rain strike the same open window from the orchard. The body did not fall at nine.

Orlo asks whether Perrin has certified the exact minute.

Elian says no. “We do not need an exact minute. We need the order: wound, bleeding, rain, bell. Three places preserve it.”

The second proposition is the clock. Fen demonstrates that the mechanism runs when the blue chalk is removed. The lower glass fell after blood was present. Merrit's receipt shows the clock had kept reliable time. The matching school fracture shows where the wedge came from.

Orlo says school chalk passes through every household.

Pella agrees. “The chalk alone does not identify you. It proves that the clock was staged with a material kept at your place of alibi.”

The third proposition is entry and route. The hall lock was not forced. Orlo had an authorized key. The garden door was pulled closed from outside. A pre-rain crossing leads to the school wash yard. Lime beneath the mud matches the map-room sill. The dry route fits the missing interval.

Fen states plainly that the footprint is not unique.

The fourth proposition is the alibi. Pella reads the separated rehearsal accounts. Four stanzas were completed before Orlo arrived. Practice sheets, candle bands, and the neighbor's hearing support the children. Jory's side-door observation is one part of the chronology, not its foundation. The bell proves Orlo was at school at nine and nothing more.

Orlo says that frightened children have confused the order.

Sister Amity asks him which practice sheet he marked before arriving. He cannot answer without adopting a child's hand as his own.

The fifth proposition is motive. Sable presents the three child burials, absence of adult succession, ghost-hearth warrants, clean-copy authorizations, and school redemptions. She does not name one beneficiary.

A villager asks whether Orlo stole for himself.

“No,” Sable says. “The money fed and clothed children. It was still common property obtained through fictitious households, and Orlo controlled the concealment. Merrit intended to expose the scheme, recover the remaining fund, move future relief under his own office, and name the families publicly.”

Pella adds, “The children who received help are not on trial. The person who made himself the only lawful authority over their need is.”

Orlo first admits the fund and denies the killing. He says Merrit would have humiliated families for the pleasure of proving that he could. Several villagers agree aloud.

Elian does not contest Merrit's character. He presents the sixth proposition.

Orlo's key gives lawful entry. His register loan supplies the selected names. His handwriting authorizes the false hearths. His school loses the matching chalk. His key carries map-room lime. His route reaches the side yard in the missing interval. His basin holds diluted blood and Merrit's collar fiber. His cuff is washed. His statements change about both time and door. He arrives only for the last stanza. The acorn weight carries the victim's fiber.

Each fact can be explained alone. No alternate suspect explains the sequence.

Bram explains ash, thread, inheritance, and a lie, but not the school chalk, basin, or late arrival. Mara explains the envelopes and hidden ledger, but not the blow or route. Hester explains unauthorized money and procedural delay, but her bridge accounts are separate and physically complete. Cora and Perrin may have concealed matters of their own; neither is needed to make Orlo's key, fund, route, and false alibi belong to someone else.

Elian states the reconstruction:

At 8:38, Orlo reached the map room through the screened clerk's passage and used his copyist's key to plead for a sealed correction, repayment, and protection of the families' names. Merrit refused and turned for the folio. At 8:44, Orlo struck him once with the nearest brass acorn weight. He then tore out the final comparison leaves, moved the clock to nine, wedged it with school chalk, broke the glass, wiped and returned the weight, left through the north garden door, crossed before the rain, washed and burned the leaves at the school, and entered for the final stanza at about 8:54.

The last bell made his presence public after the murder was already complete.

Hester asks Orlo whether he wishes to answer.

He says Merrit would have taken food from children to increase his own office. Pella says that may be true. Orlo says no one else would fund the school. Sable says the village's failure explains why the forgery endured. It does not explain the blood in the basin.

Orlo finally says, “He turned his back as though I were already finished.”

The admission comes after the case. It explains the unplanned blow and deliberate concealment. It is not allowed to become the missing clue.

The moot finds that Orlo Vane killed Merrit Alder. Wil Sloe takes his key and remands him for the magistrate.

### The collateral findings

The village wants every secret resolved before breakfast. The Lantern Measure refuses.

Bram's desk search is referred to the estate proceeding. His murder accusation is withdrawn publicly, but the lie remains in the record.

Mara admits the brandy trade and Merrit's extortion. The moot imposes duty and a substantial fine. It does not pretend that blackmail made her a murderer or that innocence of murder legalizes the tunnel.

Hester admits the bridge expenditure. The village ratifies the completed work because the stone is in the bridge, censures her concealment, and removes unilateral emergency spending from the reeve's office.

Cora's and Perrin's matters are not decided. The Lantern Measure never visited the mill or apothecary and will not convert unused leads into findings. Their names remain among the suspects originally investigated, but no collateral judgment is entered.

### The school and the archive

The ghost hearths are closed. Pella proposes a relief fund with public totals, private recipients, and three separately held keys. Sable adds quarterly reconciliation between authorization and redemption. The village votes to continue meals, shoes, slate, and lamp oil under that structure.

Sister Amity and two rotating village tutors keep the school open. The pupils do not lose breakfast because their teacher committed murder.

Merrit's archive is divided. The murder exhibits remain for the magistrate. Civic records return to the Hearth Book, chest, chapel, and school. Unrelated leverage files are returned to the people named, destroyed by consent, or referred to a separate lawful inquiry. Merrit's death does not give the village a public right to every private wrong he collected.

The Lantern Measure leaves after the magistrate arrives. The stone bridge is still standing. The school bell rings late because no adult wants to touch the rope. At noon, one of the older pupils climbs the stool, takes it in both hands, and rings it anyway.

## Journal result

The canonical journal records:

- 156 raw events and 154 active events after one correction;
- 43 operations;
- ten visits across nine distinct encounters;
- 84 spotted clues and 39 unseen clues;
- all seventeen necessary revelations and seven optional revelations established;
- three optional suspect explanations established: Bram, Mara, and Hester;
- the optional acorn-weight conclusion established;
- Cora's and Perrin's optional explanations left unresolved;
- all eleven encounters available;
- six durable consequences; and
- one append-only correction voiding the overconfident Bram inference.

The case succeeds without visiting Moss Apothecary or Bramble Mill, without exposing the school beneficiaries, without relying on one child, and without requiring Orlo's admission.
