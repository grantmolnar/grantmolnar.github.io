# The Second Audience

**Role:** necessary, end  
**Tags:** court, imperial, finale, judgment

The final audience is dispatch, not ratification: the Emperor tests recipients, submissions, burdens, fallbacks, and the first orders that must leave before the court disperses.

## Opening View

Beyond the audience doors, a courier waits mounted. Inside, one broad sheet replaces the seven drowned tablets; primary and fallback bundles flank the submission register. Delegates kneel behind keys, standards, road tokens, ledgers, and named field recipients. Rei waits behind the low screen. The Emperor ignores the number of seals. He asks who obeys first, who pays, who may stop the holder—and which order leaves before the court disperses.

## Encounter Material

## One sheet beneath the throne

The seven water-darkened tablets no longer lie before the dais. In their place rests one unmarked sheet of Imperial paper, broad enough to receive the Mandate and still leave a generous margin beneath it. The margin is not decorative. It is the visible difference between advice and command.

The primary bundle lies at the Witnesses’ right. The fallback lies to the left under a different cord, with the submission register between them. Behind the Witnesses kneel the principal delegates, each bearing a full letter of authority, a named field recipient, and a courier route. They are arranged not by coalition but by the order in which present possession must become lawful service, transfer, or withdrawal.

Rei and one other Reedwater witness are present behind a low screen. They may speak only when addressed or when Kazetada certifies that a proposed amendment creates a new provincial burden not previously disclosed. Their limited place is itself a test of the Mandate's promised standing.

The Emperor does not ask for a tally of seals. He asks shorter questions than the court prefers: Who obeys first? Who pays? Who may stop the holder? What happens when the named successor is not ready? Clan approval matters as evidence of implementation, not as a vote granting the Throne authority it already possesses.

He requires the purpose of the Mandate in one sentence.

## Order before dispatch

The audience proceeds in six movements. This is not ratification by assembled interests; it is the last inspection before dispatch. Details may move, but the six movements should not, because their order determines whether submission precedes command, whether burdens have owners, and whether the first courier can leave with an order a named officer can receive.

### 1. Imperial purpose

State why Reedwater is being governed and why this distribution serves that purpose. Naming clan satisfaction, balance, or compromise as the purpose weakens the proposal. Those may be means. The Emperor expects the object to be his peace, his people, the next rains, lawful reconstruction, or the restoration of provincial government.

Kazetada then reads the first recital, including the services already rendered and the principle that emergency arrival created duties and claims for repayment, not private title.

### 2. Acts of submission

Tomoe calls each intervening power whose present possession must change. The delegate performs a completed act or presents a sealed schedule for one that cannot occur immediately without endangering Reedwater.

The Emperor distinguishes three kinds of obedience:

- **Immediate transfer:** keys, seals, standards, checkpoints, records, or command pass now.
- **Commissioned continuance:** the clan retains execution under newly named Imperial authority, limits, audit, term, and succession.
- **Staged replacement:** emergency possession narrows according to a funded schedule tied to replacement capacity.

A promise of later obedience leaves replacement unstaged. The schedule must name who performs the work meanwhile, what power has already ended, and what happens if the transfer condition is never certified.

### 3. The Seven Reeds

Present each Reed in the same order: title, execution, audit, resources, ordinary emergency action, extraordinary sacrifice, ordinary succession, failure succession, term, review, and remedy. The Emperor may interrupt after any field.

The court should not become seven repeated speeches. Each Reed is presented through its principal political danger.

- **Harvest:** Can the holder protect seed and households while feeding the garrison without turning scarcity into command?
- **Waters:** Can engineers act quickly without deciding alone which district the Empire sacrifices?
- **Roads:** Can movement and requisition remain reliable without making every passing need a permanent debt or every closure a private border?
- **Sword:** Can command remain real while belonging visibly to the Emperor and ending when its commission ends?
- **Judgment:** Can divided officers answer to one law when judges recuse, evidence is protected, and emergency acts need timely review?
- **Shrines:** Can sacred obligations survive reconstruction without allowing consultation to become an invisible veto?
- **Accounts:** Can inspection expose theft and error without letting secrecy or custody become final authority?

### 4. Dangerous combinations

The Emperor tests the links rather than the tablets. He asks who feeds the soldiers, who audits the collector, who can halt the engineer, who hears an appeal against the magistrate, who records compensated requisition, who protects a source while correcting a false public demand, and who acts when the named holder fails.

This movement often decides the audience. A proposal can sound reasonable Reed by Reed and still recreate a private province through dependencies.

### 5. First forty days and autumn review

Shun reads the first proclamations and transfer dates. Identify the first orders field officers receive, the stores and pay that support them, the petitions immediately available, and the first public evidence that emergency possession has ended or changed form.

The Emperor then asks what the autumn review can actually do. A review that can only praise or criticize is a ceremony. The Mandate should permit renewal, narrowing, replacement, compensation, correction, removal, or activation of failure succession according to named standards.

### 6. The fallback

State why a second proposal exists. It names the failure in the primary that would justify choosing the fallback and discloses the principal changed burdens. The Emperor may ask for the fallback even when the primary appears strong. He may use one clause from it as an amendment. He may also reject the premise that either draft exhausts his choices.

Maintaining a fallback is not disloyal if both drafts answer the same command. Concealing its supporters, costs, or dependence on unreaffirmed promises is.

## The Emperor's questions

The Emperor's questions are not riddles. They expose what the court has chosen to value. Use those most relevant to the proposed design.

### Questions of authorship

- Which power is held because We appoint it, and which is merely described after the clan already took it?
- What does the strongest beneficiary surrender because this is Our peace rather than its victory?
- Which recital thanks service without making service a source of title?
- If every clan denied making a bargain, which clauses would still stand as Imperial commands?

### Questions of obedience

- What order reaches the field tomorrow that did not govern yesterday?
- Which officer must obey a person of another clan, and what makes that obedience honorable and enforceable?
- What possession has already been returned?
- What remains in clan hands only because replacement is not yet safe, and who certifies that safety?
- What happens if the current holder declares the emergency unfinished?

### Questions of burden

- Which district pays most for this Mandate?
- Which households are called by more than one Reed?
- Which promise consumes rice, timber, horses, labor, or land already promised elsewhere?
- Who can petition before the burden becomes irreversible?
- What compensation is a debt, what is an exemption, and what is merely an apology?

### Questions of constraint

- Who may inspect the inspector?
- Who may judge when the judge recuses?
- Who may stop an engineer without assuming the engineer's work?
- Which secret must become public before final authority is exercised?
- Which dangerous combination remains, and why is its term short enough to be borne?

### Questions of failure

- Who acts if the titleholder dies tonight?
- Who acts if the executor is accused by the auditor?
- Which successor inherits records and resources rather than only a name?
- What order controls when two lawful messengers disagree?
- Which clause prevents the next flood from recreating seven vacancies?

### Questions of transition

- Which temporary commission ends by date, and which ends by capacity?
- Who trains the provincial successor, and what happens if training is withheld?
- What must be true at autumn review for renewal?
- Which emergency debt survives the office that created it?
- What does Reedwater govern for itself at the end of the term that it cannot govern on the first day?

## The acts of submission

The audience should include at least three visible acts, but no act should be empty theater. Select those corresponding to the actual settlement. Every act has a court instrument and a field counterpart: an authenticated order, named receiving officer, effective hour, inventory or roster, acknowledgment route, and incomplete-transfer remedy. The object before the throne changes legal claim; the matched order changes command in Reedwater.

### White keys

A Crane steward may return granary keys to an Imperial or provincial titleholder while receiving a sealed copy of the supply covenant and recognition of relief debts. The inventory must distinguish seed, household relief, military allotment, and emergency reserve. If the stores are not inventoried, the keys transfer blame more readily than custody.

### Red standards

A Lion commander may lower a field standard and receive it back beneath an Imperial commission. The recommission names the chain of command, garrison size, supply, district replacement schedule, and the date on which independent emergency command ends. Lowering without changed orders is pageantry. Recommission without lowering may look like reward for mobilization.

### Open ledger

A Scorpion custodian may deliver sealed Accounts records to outside custody while preserving a protected-source register under divided access. The act separates custody, inspection, reporting, and sanction. If final findings may remain permanently secret, the delivery merely changes the room in which authority hides.

### Road token

A Unicorn officer may return checkpoint tokens and receive route execution under a commission with bounded requisition, valuation, repayment, and closure powers. The first proclamation must tell travelers which demands are lawful and where a correction may be sought.

### Engineer's rule

A Crab engineer may lay the measuring rule before the Imperial titleholder, then receive it back for execution of an approved plan. The plan names ordinary site safety, extraordinary sacrifice, materials, labor, sacred consultation, relocation, and the official who owns political choices the engineer did not make.

### Shrine register

A Phoenix commissioner may present the register of sacred sites together with the training and transfer schedule for local keepers. The act honors the duties preserved while demonstrating that sacred expertise does not create permanent foreign custody.

### Petition cord

A Dragon magistrate may tie a separate cord to the Judgment seal, naming the substitute who acts on recusal and the route by which provincial petitioners reach review. The cord is cut only if the Mandate abandons outside appeal.

## Amendment from the throne

The Emperor may amend the proposal. His authority does not make consequences disappear. When he changes a material term, Kazetada names the dependent clauses and public promises affected; Harunori names contradictions and remedies; Shun names proclamations that must change; Tomoe names acts of submission that must be reaffirmed; the provincial witness names any new burden.

Answer an amendment in four ways:

1. **Accept and conform:** revise dependent clauses and state the cost.
2. **Accept with a narrower safeguard:** preserve the Imperial objective while limiting duration, scope, or dangerous combinations.
3. **Invoke the fallback:** show that the fallback answers the Emperor's concern with fewer contradictions or a different burden.
4. **Record an objection:** fulfill giri by explaining the predicted failure without refusing obedience.

The audience is not a contest in which contradicting the Emperor is success. Neither is silent agreement the only honorable response. The temporary office exists because the Throne asked for witnessed judgment. A precise objection may be the last service that office can render.

## Six durable outcomes

No outcome is the hidden correct Mandate. Each can govern Reedwater if its capacities and costs are honestly supplied. Use the actual clauses rather than forcing the proposal into one label.

### 1. The Imperial Spine

Imperial families hold most public titles, appeals, and failure succession. Great Clan specialists retain bounded execution under commissions, audits, fixed terms, and visible transfer schedules.

The Imperial Spine strongly restores Imperial authorship and makes mobilization less likely to create formal title. It depends on enough Imperial clerks, heralds, guards, and deputies to carry the spine. If those resources are fictitious, Great Clan executors will become the real government while Imperial title absorbs blame.

Likely supporters include Kazetada, Tomoe, Shun, clans receiving meaningful execution, and witnesses who value a clear petition route. Likely opponents are delegations asked to carry work without public status or secure renewal.

The first crisis tests whether Imperial title can issue a timely order rather than merely approve one.

### 2. Paired Custodies

The Mandate deliberately pairs rival or unlike interests: Crane Harvest with Lion supply and Sword; Crab Waters with Phoenix consultation and Dragon review; Unicorn Roads with Accounts valuation; Scorpion inspection with outside custody and Miya correction.

Paired Custodies make reciprocal relinquishment the basis of stability. It is resilient when each pair has a tie-breaker, outside remedy, and resource schedule. It becomes brittle when every action requires unanimous cooperation or when one partner can starve the other of records, rice, labor, or proclamation.

Likely supporters are factions receiving a bounded power they can defend as real. Likely opponents are those who consider shared work a public admission that a rival's claim is legitimate.

The first crisis tests whether the pairs can act before every grievance is settled.

### 3. The Reedwater Succession

Temporary clan and Imperial commissions are explicitly transitional. Provincial officers, shrine keepers, clerks, engineers, road stewards, and magistrates receive training, resources, records, and staged authority. Autumn review measures transfer rather than merely performance by outside executors.

The Reedwater Succession gives the province its strongest long-term legitimacy and reduces permanent occupation. It is the hardest to implement immediately. Local capacity is uneven, village interests conflict, and a provincial successor can become the client of whichever clan funds training and stores.

Likely supporters include Rei, Saburo, Hojun, Kenta, Shun, and delegations willing to convert emergency service into honored completion. Likely opponents are executors who distrust local capacity or expect transition conditions never to be met.

The first crisis tests whether the new local officer is allowed to fail, learn, and still retain lawful authority.

### 4. The Reconstruction Directorate

For a short and explicit term, Waters, Roads, labor coordination, materials, and selected emergency powers are joined under an Imperially titled reconstruction directorate. Harvest, Sword, Shrines, Judgment, Accounts, and provincial standing remain outside checks with strong failure succession and a hard sunset.

The Reconstruction Directorate has the greatest capacity before the next rains. It also creates the most dangerous concentration of power and burden. Its legitimacy depends on named political sacrifice authority, a cumulative burden ledger, independent records, fast appeal, and a sunset that cannot be certified solely by the directorate.

Likely supporters include engineers, logisticians, military officers concerned with timing, and courtiers who fear paralysis. Likely opponents are displaced districts, sacred communities, and anyone who believes temporary concentration in Rokugan tends to acquire descendants.

The first crisis tests whether the directorate reports a failure that would justify narrowing its own authority.

### 5. The Honored Occupation

The current emergency powers remain in place for one harvest under explicit Imperial commissions. The Mandate replaces private claims with public terms, supply, audit, compensation, submission, and a fixed demobilization sequence, but it does not attempt immediate structural transformation.

The Honored Occupation minimizes disruption and may preserve lives through the next season. It risks teaching that mobilization is rewarded so long as the resulting occupation is competently regularized. Its honor depends on visible surrender before recommission, no automatic renewal, and real replacement work beginning immediately.

Likely supporters are current executors, districts dependent on existing relief chains, and officials skeptical of untested successors. Likely opponents are Kazetada, clans excluded from present possession, and witnesses who already experience emergency burdens as permanent.

The first crisis tests whether an executor treats the Mandate as a limit or as recognition of what it had already won.

### 6. The Narrow Imperial Peace

The Emperor rejects much of the architecture and issues a narrower order: immediate military submission, temporary continuation of essential relief, a small set of divided offices, and a new commission to complete the rest after the next rains.

The Narrow Imperial Peace may prevent clan war without solving Reedwater's government. It can still be a partial success if it closes the most dangerous claims, protects seed and reconstruction, preserves provincial standing, and creates a lawful deadline for unfinished work. It becomes failure if delay merely gives temporary holders another season to entrench.

The Witnesses may lose patronage because preferred offices remain unresolved. It may also preserve the province from an elegant Mandate whose capacities were invented.

The first crisis tests whether the unfinished commission can deliberate without the emergency settlement becoming the new normal.

## Failure without collapse

The Emperor may reject the primary, choose the fallback, or impose an amendment that damages a patron. None automatically means the commission failed. Their giri was to bring a governable judgment, expose costs, and prevent emergency possession from becoming invisible law.

Conversely, adoption as written is not automatic success. A beautiful Mandate with unfunded successors, cumulative labor demands, decorative submission, or inaccessible remedies may fail in Reedwater while winning every courtly appearance.

Judge the outcome across six dimensions:

- **Authorship:** Does the settlement remain recognizably Imperial?
- **Obedience:** Have present holders changed command, custody, or legal status?
- **Capacity:** Can the named actors perform the work with assigned resources?
- **Constraint:** Can dangerous powers be inspected, stopped, appealed, and succeeded?
- **Burden legitimacy:** Are costs named, cumulative, contestable, and compensated where promised?
- **Transition:** Does the arrangement end, mature, or transfer through observable conditions?

A Mandate may be strong in four dimensions and deliberately weak in two. Record those weaknesses as future political material rather than hiding them behind a single success grade.

## Imperial issuance

After questions and amendments, the Emperor writes or dictates one of five dispositions:

- adopted substantially as presented;
- adopted with named amendments;
- primary joined to one or more fallback clauses;
- fallback adopted with amendments; or
- narrow Imperial peace issued while the larger settlement is returned for later work.

He then commands the first acts, not merely the final seals. Before the court disperses, a messenger is already running. A standard lowers, a key changes hands, an order is copied, a burden ledger is opened, and an engineer receives authority beneath a name not his own.

The final image should belong to the consequence chosen. The unmarked margin may remain small, showing that the drafted work endured. It may contain one decisive line that rearranges a coalition. It may be filled with Imperial corrections. Or the Emperor may set the broad sheet aside and write a shorter order on fresh paper.

Whatever the disposition, the seven old tablets are not returned to their places. The flood ended that government. What leaves the hall is not its restoration, but the first order of the thing that must grow after it.

## What to record

- the stated Imperial purpose;
- the primary and fallback architectures;
- acts of submission completed, scheduled, or refused;
- every amendment and the dependencies it reopened;
- the Emperor’s questions the Witnesses could not answer;
- the adopted disposition and first-forty-days orders;
- patrons honored, disappointed, or exposed;
- burdens accepted by Reedwater and remedies preserved;
- dangerous combinations knowingly retained; and
- the first political crisis implied by the chosen outcome.
