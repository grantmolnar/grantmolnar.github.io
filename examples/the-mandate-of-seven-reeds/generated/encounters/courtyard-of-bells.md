# Courtyard of Bells

**Role:** necessary  
**Tags:** court, unicorn, roads, trade

Every bell marks a road, a delay, or a taking. Ide Sachiko can make Reedwater move if priority is singular, requisition becomes valued debt, closures preserve one minimum route, and households survive the map drawn across them.

## Opening View

Four bells announce four claims upon one road: remount, freight, gate passage, requisition. Ide Sachiko records the debt of each taking while Shinjo Boroldai counts the day a delayed dispatch will lose. Iuchi Nergui lays a household tally across the faster ferry line. The team stands harnessed, but the village cart’s owner grips the shaft. When the bells fall silent, no wheel has received lawful priority.

## Encounter Material

## A road heard before it is seen

The bells are a working map. One note marks a messenger entering from the northern post road, two quick notes a remount change, a low bronze voice a freight wagon too heavy for the inner bridge, and a hard little chime a cart traveling under requisition seal. Ide Sachiko identifies the route of an arriving rider without turning her head. “A road speaks before the traveler does,” she says, and rings in three couriers for the Witnesses to hear.

The first rides a Unicorn horse and carries an Imperial packet. The second leads an exhausted village mare requisitioned by a Lion patrol. The third walks because the cart animal promised to him at the western station was taken two days earlier by a Crane grain convoy. All three journeys are lawful under one emergency order or another. Together they prove that the road is open only in the ceremonial sense.

Sachiko does not ask for ownership of the roads. She asks who will decide which journey must wait, who may take an animal or cart, who records the taking, and who restores the local capacity consumed by Imperial service.

## The Roads Reed is not one power

Court language tends to compress Roads into bridges and post stations. Sachiko divides the Reed into nine powers that may be held separately.

1. **Title:** who speaks in the Emperor's name for the road system.
2. **Route planning:** who sets ordinary routes, closures, and seasonal alternatives.
3. **Execution:** who maintains stations, ferries, bridges, remounts, and schedules.
4. **Priority:** who decides whether messengers, grain, troops, labor, or private carts move first.
5. **Requisition:** who may take animals, carts, boats, fodder, and lodging for public use.
6. **Valuation and repayment:** who records what was taken, assigns its value, and pays or credits the owner.
7. **Tolls and customs:** who may collect revenue or inspect cargo without turning inquiry into seizure.
8. **Emergency closure:** who may close a route, for how long, and what alternative must remain.
9. **Succession and review:** who continues the network if an executor is removed, absent, or compromised.

A grant that assigns only title and execution leaves the most coercive powers in the margins. A grant that gives one office priority, requisition, tolls, closure, and customs can make movement depend on obedience to a private government.

## Three Unicorn positions

### Ide Sachiko: a road must survive its bargain

Sachiko built the western relief route by joining caravan associations, village relays, remount herds, temple lodging, and emergency warrants issued by four different powers. Her accomplishment is political rather than merely logistical: people who did not trust one another accepted the same sequence because each knew what the next station had promised.

She seeks Unicorn execution of Roads beneath a Miya title for one year and one planting season. She treats a road as a promise repeated at every gate, and her questions always travel outward: what token leaves the capital, what order reaches the station, what receipt returns, and what animal remains in the household afterward. She accepts Scorpion inspection of common manifests, Dragon review of disputed tolls and requisitions, and an autumn review. She will not accept several offices issuing independent priority orders to the same station.

Sachiko's preferred concession is to shorten Unicorn tenure while leaving the remount chain intact under named local successors. Her red line is a general phrase such as “necessary transport may be taken.” She has watched those words consume three animals in succession from the same household because each officer treated the previous taking as someone else's affair.

Sachiko exerts pressure by making every proposed clause travel. She asks where it begins, which gate hears it, who has the next horse, what happens when the bridge is out, and what receipt arrives at the household that paid for the journey.

### Shinjo Boroldai: speed, security, and the right to refuse delay

Boroldai commands the riders who linked the western road after the flood. He has no appetite for toll policy and little interest in formal title. He wants one person able to clear a route when delay kills.

His riders carried dike measurements, warnings of fever, seed tallies, and orders to release grain. They also carried clan demands dressed as emergency messages. Boroldai learned that a priority system with no hierarchy merely gives the fastest horse to the highest-status petitioner standing nearby.

He proposes four ordinary priority classes: Imperial dispatch, immediate danger to life or works, scheduled relief and seed transport, and ordinary official or private movement. A Sword commander may elevate military traffic for seven days under a recorded emergency, but the order must state what other traffic is displaced. Waters may claim emergency passage for crews and materials, but not close the route after the convoy has passed. Shrines may request protected movement for displaced communities and sacred objects, but the request must specify people, destination, and duration.

Boroldai will place Unicorn riders under Miya dispatch and Seppun emergency certification. He will even accept a local provincial captain as his successor. He will not accept six offices issuing independent priority orders to the same station keeper. His red line is not outside control; it is simultaneous command.

He exerts pressure with the remount board. Every new priority moves a wooden marker and visibly delays another journey. Everything can be preserved only by inventing more horses.

### Iuchi Nergui: crossings, households, and routes that are more than lines

Nergui supervised the western ferries and temporary bridges. She knows which crossings are engineering works, which depend on local rites, which exist only because particular households maintain boats, and which cease to be safe when floodwater changes the river's temperament.

She does not claim Shrines. She insists that Roads recognize the obligations on which a crossing rests. If a bridge replaces a ferry, the ferrymen lose both income and the duty by which their community understands itself. If a route moves around a flooded shrine, the temporary road may pass through burial ground or winter pasture. If the Crab northern channel cuts the west road, the substitute route requires two new stations, eighty additional horses, and a ford that cannot carry stone wagons during rain.

Nergui wants route decisions to include a crossing record: the physical work, the community that maintains it, the ritual or seasonal condition, the burden created by relocation, and the successor if the arrangement fails. She can accept Phoenix consultation and Kaiu engineering judgment. She opposes any rule in which either office may describe the whole road question in its own vocabulary.

Her red line is a map with no households on it. Her pressure is one question: who remains at the crossing after the court’s temporary executor rides home?

## The requisition chain

A valid requisition is not permission to take whatever is useful. The road network survives only if each taking passes through a chain.

1. **Authority:** the official cites the ordinary schedule or a named emergency order.
2. **Identification:** the receipt names the animal, cart, boat, lodging, fodder, or labor taken.
3. **Condition:** a witness records its condition before service.
4. **Valuation:** the schedule assigns a value or a method for later valuation.
5. **Return or loss:** the receiving station records return, injury, consumption, or loss.
6. **Debtor:** Roads, Sword, Waters, Harvest, or another named office accepts the debt.
7. **Payment date:** Accounts pays, credits tax, or records a scheduled installment.
8. **Appeal:** the owner may challenge identity, condition, valuation, or nonpayment without surrendering future access to the road.

The chain is deliberately bureaucratic. The alternative is not simplicity. The alternative is a village learning that lending one horse invites every later officer to call the household already participating.

Sachiko will accept compensation partly through tax credit, replacement animals, fodder grants, or priority access to trade. Saburo warns that these forms are not interchangeable. A merchant association can survive delayed coin. A household with one remaining plow animal cannot eat a future customs exemption.

## Tolls, customs, and inspection

Sachiko accepts a public toll schedule at major repaired crossings. She prefers no toll on Imperial messages, seed rice, shrine relocation, or laborers traveling under Waters orders. Grain convoys, merchant freight, and military baggage may pay differentiated rates if the rate funds the road rather than the officeholder.

The Scorpion may inspect common manifests and compare station copies. Inspectors may detain cargo for a fixed interval when seals conflict, but they may not set the toll, reroute ordinary traffic, or convert an inquiry into permanent seizure. Judgment hears disputes. Accounts preserves the debt and revenue records. Roads keeps the traffic moving while those offices perform their narrower functions.

A road with no inspection becomes a corridor for invisible privilege. A road where inspection can stop every rival becomes a weapon. The Mandate must preserve both movement and comparison.

## Closures and the minimum open route

Every closure must state:

- the physical or military cause;
- the authority ordering it;
- the affected traffic;
- the alternative route or an explanation that none exists;
- the hour of review; and
- the person empowered to reopen the road.

Boroldai proposes a **minimum open route** rule: unless the Emperor or a Seppun-certified emergency expressly suspends it, every district must retain one route for messages, one route for food, and one route for petitions. These may be the same road. They may not all disappear into a general closure whose author is also the beneficiary.

The rule constrains the Unicorn as much as the Lion. Sachiko supports it because a network becomes legitimate when its executor is forbidden to make rescue depend on renewed bargaining.

## Road duties the Mandate can place

### Miya title, Unicorn execution, local succession

Miya holds Roads in the Emperor's name. Sachiko executes the network for one year and one planting season. Boroldai serves under Miya dispatch, common manifests go to Accounts, and two provincial successors are trained at every district station. The autumn review can shorten, extend, or localize the commission.

### Rotating execution with one dispatch law

Crane barges, Unicorn remounts, and provincial road officers execute by district or season, but all obey one priority schedule, requisition chain, and closure rule. Sachiko accepts less office if the court does not recreate three incompatible roads beneath one title.

### Roads in exchange for bounded sacrifice

The Unicorn will accept the northern-channel design cutting the present west road if Waters funds the substitute stations, provides labor for the ford, and records the transport capacity lost during construction. The exchange is not “Roads yields to Waters.” It is a priced political decision about which district waits and who restores what was consumed.

### Public relinquishment of toll privilege

At the moon-viewing, Sachiko can surrender all emergency Unicorn toll seals into Miya custody. In return, the Mandate must adopt a proclaimed toll schedule and recognize valid requisition debts. The gesture transforms apparent private profit into visible submission without pretending the network was free.

## Failure modes

- **Roads without valuation:** every journey consumes the next season's animals and carts.
- **Priority without hierarchy:** status at the station replaces law.
- **Closure without alternatives:** commanders can manufacture dependence by obstructing rivals.
- **Customs with sanction:** inspectors become road governors without receiving the title.
- **Local succession without resources:** provincial officers inherit empty stables and ceremonial seals.
- **Compensation without dates:** every taking is acknowledged and none is repaid.

## What to record

- who holds title, execution, priority, requisition, toll, closure, and review;
- the ordinary priority hierarchy and emergency variance;
- the requisition receipt, valuation, debtor, and payment schedule;
- which traffic is exempt from tolls;
- how customs inspection is bounded;
- the minimum open route for messages, food, and petitions;
- the road consequences of the chosen Waters design; and
- the names, resources, and training of local successors.
