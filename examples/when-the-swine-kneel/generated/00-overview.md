## Synopsis

Three days into Veyr's uninterrupted drought pumping, pigs across Rillcross refuse particular channels, root bronze survey pins from the soil, and kneel toward the city at the sixth evening bell. The same pulse reaches thousands of animals inside Southgate as condemnation, quarantine, and evidence-seizure orders move toward execution. The Hall of Condemnations grants an independent company a resonance cycle to preserve the witnesses, trace the buried warning, and return with a choice the city can lawfully carry out.

## Premise

Veyr has mistaken a living warning for livestock disorder. The final drought schedule drives an old civic resonator beneath the city beyond tolerance; frightened pigs reveal its six buried lines more clearly than any surviving instrument. Water officers, slaughterers, quarantine officials, farmers, and the College each hold part of the evidence and a reason to control it. A bonded company receives a narrow inspection writ: keep the case intact, identify the danger before the next sixth-bell alignment, and bring the Hall an executable remedy with its costs named.

## Explanation

The Deep Bell is an ancient bronze resonator suspended above an unstable hollow and the buried river. Six sounding lines run from its chamber beneath Veyr's edge and outward through Rillcross; Southgate's old weighing works stand above the urban station on the third line. The pigs were never bred or trained as instruments. Their low bodies and sensitivity to ground-borne pressure make them the best surviving indicators: kneeling marks the Bell's central phase, water refusal marks a pulse traveling through a channel, and rooting exposes a sounding pin ringing out of time.

Nine-Mile is a modern pump station built onto an auxiliary hydraulic branch of that forgotten system. Its intermittent drought schedule strained the hollow for weeks without forcing failure. Three days before the Hall hearing, Master of Waters Corven Dast ordered uninterrupted high-load operation through the Convocation of Grain. The first full evening cycle produced the farm warnings, a dangerous College trace, and injuries among the lower pump crews. Under-surveyor Tamar Vey matched the pulse to Nine-Mile and urged a staged reduction. Dast knew that rationing would begin before the reservoirs held a full day of ordinary supply; he also knew that the load was striking buried civic works beyond tolerance. He did not possess the surveyors' lost six-line doctrine.

Once the warning became measurable, Dast treated custody as indemnity. He impounded the trace, confined Tamar, ordered field pins removed before farmers and College staff could map the network, fitted damped faces to the gauges, and raised the quota again. A reduced schedule would require a signature beneath the wards and cisterns it deprived; he preferred altered instruments and unwarned farms to an order bearing his name. He will concede a reading that survives scrutiny, then require the challenger to name the hospital, fire main, tenement, or lower crew placed at risk by acting on it. The next full-load sixth-bell alignment will drive the Bell into cascading failure unless load, phase, or pressure is changed. The investigation can reunite the divided technical record, keep live readings and trained crews in place, and return four severe choices to public authority: ration and brace, retune the six lines, vent a controlled collapse, or break the warning for a few deceptive hours of silence.
