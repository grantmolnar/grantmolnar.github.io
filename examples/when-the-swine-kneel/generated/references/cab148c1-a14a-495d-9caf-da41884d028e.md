## Reference Material

## A marker, not a control

The First Survey set bronze sounding pins at surface stations along the Deep Bell's six lines. A pin couples buried vibration to soil, paving, scales, memorial work, or another known point above. Under strain it may tremble, hum, or ring out of phase. Pigs often expose a bad station by rooting at the metal before people can feel the pulse.

A single pin proves little without provenance and comparison. Its mark can identify the Survey, its socket can show recent removal, and its response can be compared with water refusal, herd movement, College traces, pump phases, and reports from other lines. Several timed stations reveal geometry that no one farm or central gauge can show.

## Removing the pin hides the figure

The pins are passive. Pulling one does not relieve pressure, close a line, stop the Bell, or make the animals safe. It removes an independent surface marker and can break the chain by which outsiders compare the buried system with inhabited ground. Their evidentiary value therefore depends on location, custody, condition, and a record of who handled them.

A pin matters where it is found and through witnessed handling. Its socket, condition, ringing, location, custody, comparison, damage, and legal effect remain current evidence.
