## Synopsis

Three evenings before Tomas Vale marries Neris Reed-in-Rain, Mera Quill opens the two sealed readings of their compact. The ceremonial lines match. The marriages in the margins do not. Tomas and Neris settled rooms, work, water, and care in ordinary speech, then let their elders choose public words large enough to honor both houses. Both households chose a formula shaped by the flood that killed Perrin Vale. The Vales hear a river bride entering the mill and keeping its dead. The river kin hear a miller entering the current and becoming answerable to warnings that once stopped at his gate. Their gifts carry the same double edge: a white veil honors the dead and buries a river bride; a closed iron ring records a brother's labor and promises captivity; a handstone offers steadiness and a fixed course. Meanwhile, twelve protected trout leak across the market because a cousin embroidered a joke until somebody believed him. The adventurers serve as free witnesses. They may question, compare, pause, and propose. They may not choose the marriage. Their task is to discover what each word, gift, duty, and public act will do, return every choice to Tomas and Neris, and sign no more than they can honestly witness. No villain opposes the wedding. Love, haste, old grief, and good intentions are enough.

## Premise

You carry the seal of the House of Open Measure and owe no debt on either bank. At the opening of the three-day witness period, Tomas Vale and Neris Reed-in-Rain's two sealed household readings are opened together. Their central words agree; their meanings do not. The wedding is set for the evening when low sun strikes the wet mill wheel. By then, two households have built different marriages out of the same compact, gifts, names, feast, and ceremony. Your office permits questions, comparison, private repetition, pauses, proposed forms, and a scoped signature. It does not permit you to decide for the couple. Move among mill, willow house, market, shrine, reed yard, music bridge, and flood meadow. Find what the old words conceal, what the new promises cost on an ordinary day, and what Tomas and Neris still choose when every beautiful thing has been explained.

## Explanation

The Concord of Banks governs agreements where mortal settlements meet the old households of rivers, woods, roads, and hills. Its House of Open Measure does not reconcile those households or make their customs interchangeable. It records the places where they touch: who speaks, who acts, who may refuse, what changes hands, and where a duty ends. The finished record must survive an unfriendly reader without becoming stronger than the people who signed it.

For a cross-bank marriage, each household files a sealed reading copy before the three-day witness period. The ceremonial lines are known in advance. The marginal gloss—what those lines confer, require, and preserve—remains sealed until both copies are opened together. Neither side can revise its reading after seeing the other. The rule prevents opportunistic editing; it also lets an honest contradiction appear late. Mera Quill received both filings and will draft the final folio. She cannot independently certify the record she kept and wrote, so the House appoints free witnesses with no kinship, lease, inheritance, debt, or old-flood quarrel on either bank.

Tomas Vale runs the Cresswater mill with his widowed sister Mara. He thinks in gates, shifts, rooms, repairs, and notices. He expects the eastern rooms to become the couple's ordinary home. Neris Reed-in-Rain keeps spawning records and flood warnings along the lower channels. She expects to share those rooms while retaining the willow house as a home whose duties may call her without permission. Tomas calls this one household in two dwellings. Neris calls it two continuing households joined by marriage. Their keys and schedules nearly agree. Their nouns do not, and those nouns govern recognition, care, authority, and future claims.

The compact's old formula is bound to the flood that killed Perrin Vale. The Vales hear it bringing Neris into the mill's living and dead without conveying the mill away from them. The river household hears it binding Tomas to warnings that once died at the wheelhouse threshold. Each side chose the same words because each believed those words repaired the flood's unfinished duty. The contradiction is not a mistranslation. It is one grief carrying two remedies.

The families prepare with materials they trust. Agnes Vale brings a white flax veil that welcomed brides and later covered her drowned husband. Serein-at-Alderfall asks Tomas to enter river kinship because a warning that stops at the mill gate is useless. Orren Underbridge has spent six nights forging a closed iron ring. Mara offers a handstone while keeping deed, wages, water lease, and succession in separate books. Rain-at-Noon carries the cracked bell from the flood and wants a warning handoff that works when the grieving elders are absent. None is secretly hostile. Each may be generous while asking the couple to inhabit a meaning they never chose.

The calendar creates pressure, not authority. The wedding may be postponed. Food can be served, platforms stored, gifts returned, and another date chosen. The joining light will not return for a year, and disappointment will harden into public stories, but postponement kills nobody.

At the Market of Seven Baskets, hospitality becomes names, appetites, chairs, beds, labor, sources, gifts, abstentions, and debts. Pella Nine-Spoons refuses to cook for “river folk” as one imaginary stomach. Protected trout must return to the channel. Deep-Reed needs a dry chair with moving water at her feet; Noll Ferry needs a dry bed; Lilt-before-Storm needs to hear current but not sleep in it. Shared food may arrive in separate courses. Attendance, eating, dancing, and silence never become assent by implication.

At the Shrine of the Open Door, Keeper Ansel reduces the ceremony to six acts: consent, household standing, names, exchange, promises, and departure. Dessa Reedwright gives those acts footing, sight lines, handrails, water return, named labor, and a rain route. Iven Crossbow and Moss-in-Evening decide who leads, who answers, where four and six meet, and where music must stop. A veil, ring, boat, refrain, or procession has no office until its giver declares one and its receiver accepts it.

The flood-meadow rehearsal puts every elegant plan under weight. Chairs arrive. Children move markers. Water cuts another channel. Food waits. Agnes cannot hear in the wheelhouse. Deep-Reed cannot turn where the drawing says she should. The old flood is rebuilt from high-water cuts, a cracked bell, delivery marks, a lost path, and duties nobody had agreed to receive. The result may be a three-signal warning chain, separate true statements, a later hearing, or no joint act. Forgiveness is not a wedding supply.

The finale displays the state the table actually made. Footing shows whose body was considered. Names show what remains stable and what may change. Gifts show which intentions were accepted, altered, withheld, or returned. Silence marks the places where no crowd may answer. The departure shows whether the threshold boat receives Tomas into kinship, removes him from the mill, or has no place at all. The folio separates consent, household standing, property, office, adoption, care, exchange, promises, open matters, adaptations, abstentions, and the scope of each signature.

A successful ending may remain rough. Tomas and Neris may keep two household descriptions beside one working schedule. Agnes and Serein may sign different lines. River adoption may be bounded or deferred. A gift may stay home. A grievance may move to another hearing. The couple may proceed without House certification, postpone, or end the process. The witnesses succeed when no difference hides inside a shared word and every remaining edge belongs to someone who knowingly chose it.

Keep four structures distinct at the table. The calendar determines when rehearsal and wedding occur. The clue graph locates evidence and examples. The authority graph shows who may commit a person, office, property, labor, or household. The ceremony state records what Tomas and Neris have accepted. A skipped encounter narrows evidence; it does not lock a door. A late fact reopens only the affected act. An absent principal may be consulted, never impersonated. Each witness signs what they observed or later verified, and nothing else.
