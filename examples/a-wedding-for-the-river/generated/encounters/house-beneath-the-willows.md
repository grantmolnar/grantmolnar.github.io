# The House Beneath the Willows

**Role:** necessary  
**Tags:** river-kin, adoption, names, rings

Under the willow roots, Serein names the price of adoption, Orren opens the ring he forged, Neris defends two homes, and Rain asks for a warning that reaches a human hand.

## Opening View

Here, a name is not spoken at once. Serein waits while the ripples from your arrival travel beneath sleeping shelves and ledgers sealed under glass. Only when the water lies still does she offer welcome. The house has no walls, only willow roots, green channels, and low platforms joined by stepping stones. On the central platform sit a closed iron ring, a cracked handbell, and a little boat tied between two posts. The bell is old. The knot is new.

## Encounter Material

## Situation
Willow roots make rooms open to current and air. Mortals enter by stepping stones and low platforms; shoes stay dry only through attention. Mussel-shell baskets hang beside reed pens, silt pigments stand in stoppered jars, and ledgers rest under glass with stones on their corners.

This is Neris's household, not a river court assembled to receive outsiders. Serein orders duty stones near the adoption boat. Orren keeps the closed iron ring wrapped in rush. Rain-at-Noon has set the cracked mill bell beside the grievance stones. Deep-Reed, Brin Quickwater, and several cousins interrupt one another with the untidy freedom of kin. Here Neris is daughter, sister, current-keeper, and principal—not an envoy hired to translate everyone else.

For every prepared object or duty, ask what changes after the ceremony and what the household owes in return. Who carries a warning when Neris is absent? Who may command dangerous work? What counts as home? Which name survives in a mortal index when her byname changes? The water makes every answer sound fluid. Find the hand that must carry it.

## Serein-at-Alderfall and exact adoption
Alder seeds cling to Serein's hair while she places six duty stones in a row. She does not want a ceremonial son or a promise that the mill will vaguely “honor the river.” She wants recognized warnings to reach somebody already bound to answer them.

Serein states the boundary before anyone asks: Tomas remains Vale kin, keeps property and trade, lives chiefly at the mill, and may refuse danger beyond the duties he undertakes. The adoption gives no command over Mara, Elsin, workers, grain, machinery, or future children.

Asked whether she wants Tomas to belong to her, Serein says, “No. I want his answer to travel farther than his gate, and ours to reach him before the yard is water.”

## Adoption without possession

River adoption does not normally require permanent residence. A person adopted by a current may still keep another hearth, trade, office, lineage, and property succession. The obligation is practical: answer flood calls, protect spawning closures, carry warnings downstream, accept rescue, and submit disputes concerning the water to a mixed hearing before using force.

Orren explains this badly. He says, “The river will know where Tomas belongs,” which sounds to mortals like a custody claim. What he means is that Tomas should no longer be treated as an outside lessee whose obligations stop at the wheel gate.

The household can offer Tomas a bounded adoption with six duties:

1. recognize three agreed warning signals: alder-seed braid for rising water, three struck shells for a sudden gate failure, and a black reed knot for spawning closure;
2. stop the wheel and open the lower sluice when the specified flood mark is reached;
3. ring the mill bell and help evacuate workers, tenants, and animals;
4. prevent employees and guests under his authority from violating a posted closure;
5. carry or relay a warning when he can do so without abandoning people already in danger; and
6. accept rescue and attend a mixed hearing before force, seizure, or deliberate obstruction in a water dispute.

The river household owes reciprocal duties:

1. deliver warnings through an agreed signal and, when possible, a named messenger;
2. give Tomas and Mara access to current readings relevant to mill operation;
3. aid evacuation rather than limiting rescue to river kin;
4. provide food, shelter, and recovery after service or injury;
5. hear a claim that a warning was late, unclear, or impossible to obey; and
6. never conceal a closure or threshold in order to test Tomas's loyalty.

Adoption does not transfer Tomas's property, cancel his mortal kinship, impose unrestricted labor, require him to enter deep current, grant Serein control of his work schedule, or assign future children to the river household.

Tomas must decide whether he accepts these duties. Neris must decide whether she accepts a marriage in which Tomas may remain most nights at the mill and sometimes fail at a river obligation without that failure becoming proof that he never belonged.

## Neris in her own household
At the mill, people mistake Neris for the river's single speaking mouth. Beneath the willows she is a daughter, sister, niece, current-keeper, and sometimes the youngest person in the room. Question her where no relative can hear.

Neris wants Tomas adopted. She does not want to become his interpreter, guarantor, or excuse. River officers should address him directly; mill workers should heed her warning because she holds the office, not because she married the miller. She also rejects the cousins' assumption that pregnancy, injury, grief, or quarrel would automatically return her to willow custody.

Most ordinary meals, possessions, and guests will gather in the eastern rooms. Neris still refuses to call the willow house a former home or to ask permission when its duties call. She says **two households joined by marriage**. Tomas says **one household in two dwellings**. Their keys and schedules may agree. Do not call the difference harmless merely because no deed turns on it.

Alone, Neris says:

> I do not need Tomas to sleep in the water. I need him not to call me absent when I am where my work and my people are. I do not need the mill to become river property. I need its promises to continue when I am not beside the wheel translating them.

## Orren Underbridge and the ring
Orren unwraps six nights of work and presents a closed iron ring made from a flood-gate fitting. He meant to learn a mortal wedding form for his sister. He did not know that the same shape can mean containment, debt without outlet, and a promise unable to breathe among his own kin. Neris will not wear it, and has delayed saying so because every hammer mark is her brother's care.

Orren can open the ring into a spiral, divide it into clasps, leave a rush-bound gap, set it into a threshold, or keep metal off Neris's body. He will not melt the work merely so the mistake vanishes. A witness who works beside him hears the fear beneath that refusal: “They will praise the river at the wedding and curse the first closure that costs them flour.”

## Names that move

Neris's childhood name was **Neris Minnow-Follows**. She became **Neris Reed-in-Rain** after keeping the lower reed beds open during a three-day storm. Her byname may change again. Mortal law, however, needs a stable entry for inheritance, testimony, taxation, and later cross-reference.

Serein objects to “Neris Vale” because it makes her daughter's river name sound discarded. Mera Quill objects to a record that changes without trace. Both concerns can be satisfied by distinguishing a stable witness identity from current bynames and places of residence.

Neris accepts a registry entry that identifies her through parentage, witness mark, and a chain of acknowledged names. She accepts “of Vale Mill” as an address when she is acting from the eastern rooms. She does not presently choose Vale as a replacement surname. This refusal is not a rejection of Tomas or Agnes.

Tomas also faces a name question. River adoption customarily grants a byname after a deed, not at the ceremony. Several cousins have proposed comic names in advance: Tomas Flour-in-Hair, Tomas Wheel-Stops-Late, and Tomas Almost-Dry. Neris wants no ceremonial nickname imposed merely to make the symmetry look tidy. Serein agrees. If Tomas later earns and accepts a river byname, Mera can append it to his record without replacing Vale.

## Rain-at-Noon and the speaking of grievances
Rain-at-Noon places Agnes's broken market basket beside the cracked yard bell. One proves a debt can be bounded: object, creditor, repair, date. The other proves a warning is unfinished until somebody receives, understands, acknowledges, and acts.

She will keep the old flood out of the public feast interval, accept stewards, and assign it to another hearing. She will not erase the river warnings, make Perrin's death wholly a Vale fault, or perform reconciliation because a musician has found a pleasing cadence.

Her useful wedding gift is a conversion board: river marks beside bell times, one named keeper on each bank, and a rule for what happens when the first keeper does not answer.

## The old flood

Serein and Agnes have not spoken directly in nineteen years. During the spring flood, river messengers warned the mill to open its lower gate. Agnes's husband **Perrin Vale** stayed to save the main wheel and died when a timber struck him. Agnes remembers a warning delivered after the yard was already underwater. Serein remembers three earlier signs that the mill dismissed because each used river measures rather than bell times.

Neither woman invented her memory. The failure lay partly in translation and partly in Perrin's choice. The first alder braid was hung where no night worker knew to look. A second warning used current depth without converting it to the mill's carved yard marks. Rain finally carried a bell, by which time evacuation remained possible but the wheel was already endangered. Perrin sent others away and stayed.

Let the flood deepen the wedding without turning it into a solved mystery. A shared acknowledgment may help; forced forgiveness will not. The practical settlement is a warning protocol that works without Neris translating. Agnes may never forgive Serein. Serein may never accept blame for Perrin's last choice. Both may still recognize a better handoff.

## Care, children, and claims of return

River households do not divide care cleanly from belonging. A person rescued, fed through illness, or sheltered after grief acquires obligations of acknowledgment and later aid. Mortal relatives may hear this as a claim over the person. River kin may hear mortal guardianship documents as permission to exclude everyone not named.

Serein's first instinct is that Neris should return beneath the willows for any serious illness because the household understands river-born bodies. Mara's first instinct is that Neris's spouse should decide where the couple receives care. Neris rejects both defaults. She wants prior directions, Tomas's voice when she cannot speak, and room for river expertise without removal from the marriage household.

A care agreement may say:

- either partner may seek care in either dwelling;
- the conscious person chooses where care occurs;
- if a person cannot choose, prior written directions and the spouse's judgment govern unless immediate survival requires otherwise;
- both households receive notice and may offer named help;
- helping with care creates gratitude and reciprocal aid, not custody or property rights;
- the surviving spouse retains agreed dwelling rights without automatically inheriting unrelated household property.

Future children present the same issue in advance. Tomas and Neris agree that no child should be assigned exclusively to one bank at birth. They are open to a mortal registry entry and a river care entry, provided neither is treated as a final household choice. Both households may owe shelter, food, warning, rescue, and teaching. Immersion, apprenticeship, inheritance, naming, and permanent residence require later, separate decisions.

Serein can accept this if specific adults sign the care duties and if a future child is not prevented from learning river marks. Mara can accept it if care status does not rewrite the mill deed or displace Elsin. Orren volunteers rescue and craft teaching. Rain volunteers warning lore but refuses regular infant care, saying that even rivers know their banks.

The couple need not decide whether they will have children. They do need to prevent the compact's household language from assigning hypothetical children without their consent.

## Productive scenes

- Neris asks to be questioned alone about residence, names, children, and the possibility of postponement.
- Tomas is asked whether he accepts the six flood duties and whether he can repeat the river household's reciprocal duties.
- Serein lays six duty stones beside six reciprocal shells and removes any pair whose terms remain vague.
- Orren proudly presents the ring and watches the room become careful.
- Rain-at-Noon demonstrates grievance custom by naming the basket debt she owes Agnes.
- A cousin tries to grant Tomas a comic byname; Neris stops the ritual before the witnesses need to intervene.
- Serein asks whether a future grandchild will be “allowed to know the current.” The useful answer is not reassurance but a named teaching duty that leaves household standing open.

## Household outcomes

Leave with a durable position, even if it is not harmony.

- Serein offers a bounded adoption with public limits and reciprocal obligations.
- Tomas accepts some or all duties, asks for revision, or declines adoption while still marrying Neris.
- Neris secures two-home recognition, or accepts one-household language with explicit equal access and no abandonment by absence.
- Orren transforms the ring while preserving its material history.
- Rain assigns the old flood to a later hearing and contributes a new warning protocol.
- The household signs named care duties without claiming future children or an incapacitated spouse.

Do not require every river relative to accept one symbolic description. Serein may say one household in two dwellings while Neris says two households joined. The record can preserve both if the duties are exact.

## If the willow house is skipped or reached late

Neris does not need her household to answer whether she marries. She may retain her current name, existing river standing, willow-house home, and personal promises. Without direct river-household authority, however, the witnesses cannot record Tomas as adopted, bind reciprocal warning or rescue duties, assume Orren gives the ring, or claim Serein recognizes the marriage on behalf of the household.

The safe fallback is marriage without adoption. State Neris's retained standing, Tomas's unaltered Vale kinship, and any personal water duties he can accept without pretending to hold a river office. Put adoption and reciprocal household duties into a named later hearing or leave them explicitly uncompleted.

If Serein, Orren, Rain-at-Noon, or another river principal arrives late, each may alter only their own sphere. Serein may accept or refuse adoption; Orren may offer, transform, or withhold the ring; Rain may steward a grievance or decline a public entry. Their answers do not travel backward to invalidate unrelated functions already completed. Pause and repeat consent only when the late term changes what Tomas or Neris understood themselves to be choosing.

## Failure forward

The river household may refuse the ring, reject a fixed surname, limit or refuse the public adoption, insist on a separate grievance rite, or decline to sign a care clause. These are not breakdowns. They make the final design more explicit and may cost symmetry, time, or one family's sense of recognition.

If Tomas cannot accept the adoption duties, Neris decides whether marriage without adoption still meets her terms. If Neris cannot secure recognized access to both homes, Tomas decides whether he can change his understanding or whether the couple postpones. If care remains vague, the witnesses can exclude it from the marriage compact and schedule a later hearing rather than allowing either household's default to control silently.

## Deep-Reed’s basin chair

Serein’s elder cousin **Deep-Reed** arrives while the guest list is being discussed. Her knees no longer tolerate crouching on roots, and deep water numbs one leg. She wants a backed chair on dry matting with her bare feet resting in a shallow basin through which fresh water moves. She laughs at the market note assigning all river elders submerged seats.

Beside her, **Noll Ferry** asks for a dry shrine bed because damp air worsens an old lung injury, while **Lilt-before-Storm** says any cot will do if she can hear running water. Their needs contradict one another without either being less river-born. Serein insists that the market record their names rather than turn one accommodation into a custom for everyone.
