# The Market of Seven Baskets

**Role:** necessary  
**Tags:** market, hospitality, feast, gifts, logistics

Pella Nine-Spoons empties seven baskets onto the market tables until every claim about hospitality becomes a name, source, seat, bed, task, gift, abstention, or debt.

## Opening View

The first trout hits the floor before Pella Nine-Spoons finishes saying good morning. By the time it is back in the leaking tank, water has reached three separate purses and a guest list so corrected that several names have acquired tributaries. Seven labeled baskets hang overhead. Pella rescues her pencil with her teeth, kicks a stack of borrowed plates out of danger, and points to the open spiral of iron on the table. “Fish first,” she says. “Then relatives.”

## Encounter Material

## Situation
The market stands where the mill road, ferry path, and lower channel meet. Every wedding plan eventually arrives here as weight, heat, distance, bedding, crockery, or unpaid work. Pella Nine-Spoons has hung seven scrubbed baskets from the rafters and written one question on each: **who, from where, carried by whom, seated where, sleeping where, given for what, and still owed what**.

The immediate problem has fins. Twelve protected spawning trout circle a wicker tank beside the fountain because Brin Quickwater told Pella, in the grave tone of an archivist, that river guests eat only fish. The tank leaks. The fish must return to moving water before any other feast discussion deserves attention. Returning them introduces the market's governing rule: food has a source, taking creates an obligation, and hospitality begins before the plate.

Pella has room, labor, and fuel for a generous feast, but not for an imaginary one. The guest list counts households where it needs names. The seating plan awards rank where it needs hearing and turning room. Lodging assumes every river guest wants wet quarters. Gifts have been entered as honors before receivers know what they are asked to mean. A grievance custom has been allotted “as long as needed,” which is how soup becomes cold and resentment becomes entertainment.

Do not make the feast uniform. Make every accommodation, refusal, and abstention belong to a named person.

## Pella Nine-Spoons
Pella is broad-shouldered, flour-dusted, and rarely holds fewer than two writing spoons. She earned her byname during a winter flood, when she fed nine stranded ferrymen from one pot after making each confess what he could not eat.

She wants hot food served hot, every source named, every worker credited, every invited body able to reach a seat, and no later claim that eating meant assent. Unity purchased with unpaid labor or a dish somebody cannot eat is poor bookkeeping and worse hospitality.

Pella can shrink the feast, split courses and tables, mark abstentions, move the grievance interval, credit substitutions, and salvage a postponed day. She will not roast “probably,” seat rank above hearing or access, hold a hot course through an endless ceremonial quarrel, or let a gift enter the rite before its receiver knows its proposed office.

Pella speaks in kitchen arithmetic:

> “Give me names or give me empty benches. I cannot roast ‘probably.’”

> “A tradition with no steward is a wish. A wish does not keep soup warm.”

> “You may disagree at my table. You may not make the kitchen pretend you agreed.”

## The seven baskets

Use the baskets in any order; carry unsettled ones elsewhere. A decision is complete only when the table can name the affected people, the promised act, its steward, and what happens when the plan changes.

### First basket: names and numbers

The two family lists do not count guests the same way. The Vale list names households, assuming spouses and dependent children are included. The river list names people with standing, assuming that anyone currently carrying a shared duty may accompany them. Pella has thirty-eight certain guests, seven probable guests, and nine people whom one list treats as included while the other does not.

Do not solve this by choosing one counting custom. Build a named register. Each entry should distinguish:

- invited person;
- accompanying person or dependent;
- feast attendance;
- ceremony attendance;
- lodging needed;
- access or comfort requirement;
- food choice or abstention;
- gift or grievance role; and
- the person who confirmed the entry.

Several guests expose the danger of representative categories.

- **Agnes Vale** needs a stable chair away from grinding noise and a clear view of anyone addressing her. She does not need to be carried, seated at the ceremonial “head,” or treated as representative of mortal age.
- **Deep-Reed**, Serein’s elder cousin, cannot crouch on willow roots. She wants a backed chair on dry matting with her bare feet in a shallow moving basin. She does not want a submerged seat.
- **Noll Ferry**, a river-born boatman with an old lung injury, sleeps best in a dry shrine room and dislikes damp bedding. Assigning every river guest beneath the willows would make him ill.
- **Lilt-before-Storm**, a young current-reader, can sleep almost anywhere but wakes in panic if she cannot hear running water. A cot beside the market fountain suits her better than an honored dry chamber.
- **Aunt Sal Vale** can walk the willow platforms and is secretly eager to do so, but she wants a handrail and will deny needing it if the request is made publicly.
- Three children from the river household and two mill apprentices want to sleep in the same loft because they are building lantern boats. Their guardians must agree; the children’s enthusiasm is not sufficient authority.

A completed register may reduce the guest list, add labor, or reveal that a modest accommodation solves an apparent cultural divide. A guest may attend the ceremony but not the feast, sleep elsewhere, bring their own food, or abstain from one custom without withdrawing from the marriage.

### Second basket: food and source

The taking ledger matters more than the ingredient category. River households eat fish, grain, greens, shellfish, fruit, and meat according to source, season, obligation, and personal preference. Mortal guests are equally varied. The protected trout are wrong because of the closed reach, not because river folk never eat fish.

Pella can build a credible feast from available stores:

1. **Arrival cups and road bread.** Barley water, mint, salt, and small wheat rolls are served before the formal feast. They are hospitality for travelers, not the first shared course, so nobody must wait hungry through the grievance interval.
2. **Common-reach mussels or herb broth.** Serein’s ledger permits a limited mussel taking from the lower common reach. Guests may choose the broth without shellfish. The gatherers are named and credited.
3. **Jory Salt’s sea catch.** **Jory Salt** has smoked silver hake from a licensed coastal crew. It is acceptable to many guests, but it is not an ethically pure substitute that appeared without labor. Jory wants his crew, transport, salt, and price named.
4. **Mill grain and garden dishes.** Vale flour, onion cakes, braised roots, river beans from planted beds, and greens cut above the flood mark can feed the majority of guests without pretending to be neutral symbols.
5. **Honey, fruit, and reed sugar.** The final sweet course can be assembled cold if ceremony or grievances delay the schedule.

Pella has oven and labor limits. She can serve forty-two people one hot course at once. She can feed up to fifty if the meal uses two seatings, one hot and one cold course, or extra cooks. Every added ceremonial pause threatens a particular dish, not “the feast” in the abstract.

Jory is brisk, proud, and unimpressed by moral theater. He can expand his order if paid and if two people help clean and portion the fish. He will not accept public thanks that call his goods “the safe kind” while omitting the crew who caught them. If the witnesses approach him as a last-minute instrument of reconciliation, he says, “I sell supper. I do not sell innocence.”

For each course, name who grew, gathered, caught, traded, cooked, or released it, and what obligation follows. Pella may speak the credit, print it on small cards, or enter it in the witness record. Provenance is part of the meal: it keeps a gift from concealing a closed reach, unpaid labor, or a debt the couple never accepted.

Pella will print two notes beside the same dishes if one grand title would lie. One can name river permissions, returned obligations, and keepers; the other can name mill grain, paid labor, and household gifts. The meal need not pretend the two accounts are one account in order to feed everyone at one table.

Her cash box follows the same discipline. The mill's grain account, Tomas's personal purse, and the household wedding fund hang from separate hooks. Each has a different signer and a different remedy when it runs short. Pella will spend from an authorized purse; she will not let a wedding invoice become evidence that the marriage acquired the mill, its debts, or its lease.

### Third basket: labor and time

The wedding has more willing hands than coordinated shifts. Tomas has promised wheel coverage, platform lifting, and evening kitchen work. Neris has promised current checks, guest guidance, and source verification. If both keep every promise, neither reaches the private consent rehearsal rested.

Pella asks the witnesses to identify every task that presently depends on one of the couple:

- returning the trout;
- confirming river sources;
- moving benches;
- collecting borrowed cups;
- escorting guests over wet ground;
- supervising children near the fountain;
- carrying grievance stones;
- translating names and announcements; and
- checking the final guest count.

Most tasks can be reassigned. Mara can release two mill hands after the afternoon grind. Orren can lead platform lifting. Rain-at-Noon can verify source marks. Mera can maintain the guest register. Brin can confirm individual guest needs under supervision. Aunt Sal can collect cups and discover that practical service grants more influence than claiming precedence.

Make labor visible. Each promise should have a named worker, start time, replacement, and stopping point. Volunteers may refuse new work. The witnesses’ authority to ask questions does not make the party an unlimited labor pool, although adventurers may earn considerable goodwill by taking one difficult shift themselves.

### Fourth basket: seats, access, and table order

The existing market hall seats thirty on dry benches. Twelve additional places can be made along the fountain with removable boards and shallow water trays. Dessa can build more, but every added place costs reed panels needed for the ceremony.

The Vales imagine a head table with Agnes and the couple at its center. The river household imagines people seated by current obligation, with the person owed a duty facing the person who owes it. Neither arrangement accommodates everyone.

Pella offers several workable plans:

- **The hinge table:** Tomas and Neris sit where two long tables meet. Agnes and Serein have equal sight lines without being forced beside one another.
- **The open square:** four short tables leave the fountain visible in the center. Guests can face grievance speakers without establishing one head.
- **Two first courses, one final table:** households eat their first course in familiar groupings, then join for gifts and sweets. This preserves comfort but makes unity a later act rather than an assumed starting state.
- **Named mixed tables:** people sit near those whose needs or duties they actually share—workers near water readers, children near guardians, gift givers near consenting receivers—rather than alternating mortal and river bodies for appearance.

No elder is owed an unsafe seat. No relative is entitled to sit beside the couple merely because a family diagram puts them there. The couple may reserve seats for witnesses, musicians, stewards, and absent people whose names they wish acknowledged.

Pella's lifting rota is as particular as the seating plan. One worker steadies Deep-Reed's chair, one keeps the shallow basin flowing, and one named relief takes over before the first hot course. If any name disappears, the duty becomes open again; it does not fall automatically to 'the river household' or to whichever Vale happens to be nearest.

**Aunt Sal Vale** is delighted by every river custom until she learns that her preferred “aunt’s place” may disappear. She is sociable, observant, and capable of useful work, but she uses charm to turn assumptions into faits accomplis. She can concede any table shape if Agnes remains visible and if her own service is publicly recognized. She will not accept being told that mortal kinship is vulgar or that only river obligations deserve ceremonial placement.

### Fifth basket: lodging

Cresswater can house the invited guests, but not by sorting them into “river” and “mortal” quarters.

Available spaces include:

- eight dry cots in the mill’s cleaned eastern loft;
- six quiet beds at the shrine, two accessible without stairs;
- ten market-stall cots under weatherproof awnings near the fountain;
- eight willow-platform sleeping places with moving water below;
- four places in the ferry shed, dry but noisy at dawn; and
- household floors and spare rooms offered by named neighbors.

Assign lodging through the register. Deep-Reed needs a level route and a basin, which Dessa can provide at the market stalls. Noll Ferry needs a dry shrine bed. Lilt-before-Storm needs audible water, not a wet mattress. Agnes sleeps at home. Aunt Sal wants the adventure of a willow platform but requires a rail and a guide. The children’s shared loft requires Mara, Orren, and the assigned night guardian to agree.

Lodging creates labor: bedding must be carried, platforms checked, lamps supplied, and guests guided after dark. A promise of “both households will find room” is not complete until specific hosts accept specific guests.

### Sixth basket: gifts and declared intentions

Pella has put a red cord around the gift basket because five objects have already acquired meanings their givers did not intend.

- Agnes’s veil offers lineage and remembrance but signifies concealed breath if placed over Neris’s face.
- Orren’s closed iron ring offers durable family labor but signifies captivity without outlet.
- Mara’s commissioned handstone, cut from a retired millstone, offers steadiness and provision but can imply that Neris’s course should be fixed.
- Aunt Sal’s red cradle ribbon offers hoped-for descendants but publicly presumes children the couple has not promised.
- Jory’s cedar salt box offers preserved food and safe roads. One river cousin assumes salt means water made barren; Jory means that the couple should never be stranded without stores.

Do not adjudicate symbols as though objects have one secret correct meaning. Use a declared-gift procedure:

1. The giver names the material, maker, source, intended honor, and any duty attached.
2. The receiver paraphrases what they believe they are being offered.
3. The receiver may accept it as made, accept a transformed use, defer it, receive it privately, or refuse it.
4. Any transformation preserves the maker’s credit and states who may alter the object.
5. No surprise gift enters the public ceremony unless both Tomas and Neris accept the surprise itself.

The veil, ring, and handstone can travel to the mill, willows, yard, shrine, or rehearsal for testing. The cradle ribbon may become a cord around the compact chest, remain a private hope, or be withheld. The salt box may be paired with a water bowl and presented as stores for travel between homes. A refused gift is not automatically an insult. A giver may withdraw it rather than let the object say something false.

Orren has left Pella an invoice and two iron samples: the closed ring he first imagined and an open spiral fastened with replaceable rush. The second keeps the hammerwork visible, costs less to maintain, and can be removed without cutting iron from the wearer. The samples do not decide Neris's answer. They make the available answers concrete enough to compare.

### Seventh basket: grievances, abstentions, and the order of eating

River custom requires unresolved obligations to be named before shared food pretends that everyone is at peace. Mortal guests fear that the wedding feast will become a public accusation. Both concerns are reasonable.

Pella, Rain-at-Noon, and Mera can operate a bounded procedure.

#### Before the feast

- Guests receive arrival cups and road bread so nobody negotiates while hungry.
- Proposed grievances are submitted to Rain and Mera by noon.
- Each entry names speaker, respondent, duty, requested next act, steward, and later hearing date if needed.
- The stewards reject character attacks, claims over the couple, surprise testimony, and matters too complex to understand in one breath.
- The old flood is assigned to its separate hearing unless Agnes and Serein both request a narrowly stated public obligation.

Pella keeps two flood-year market records in the grievance packet. The delivery slate marks two river signs before the mill bell. A separate wage slate pays a porter to carry the first warning to the mill gate, but its receipt line is blank. Together they prove dispatch and route, not delivery, acknowledgment, escalation, or what Perrin chose after the water reached him.

#### The public interval

- Pella sets twelve minutes and no more than four public entries.
- Rain states the scope: unfinished obligation, not verdict on a person.
- The speaker names the duty and requested next act in one breath.
- The respondent may acknowledge it, state that it was discharged, contest it while accepting a hearing, or decline a public reply and accept private stewardship.
- The steward places the marked obligation stone in a water bowl. Washing the stone means the matter has been honestly carried into the meal, not resolved.
- When time ends, unresolved entries remain in the ledger and the first shared course begins.

Private entries remain valid. A guest may attend while abstaining from the grievance interval, one course, a toast, a dance, or a gift. Pella marks abstention with a blank reed token at the guest’s place. The token says only “present, not participating in this act.” It is not a vote against the marriage.

The limit protects Rain from a feast that lies about unfinished duties, Agnes from an ambush over Perrin's death, and Pella from holding supper for a quarrel without an end.

## Brin Quickwater and accountable comedy

**Brin Quickwater** is Neris’s cousin, a charming idler with a gift for delivering inventions in the tone of an archivist. He has claimed that river guests eat only fish, that odd-numbered cups invite drought, and that a groom must be addressed by the first object he drops. He does not intend harm. He expects adults to recognize play and is genuinely startled when Pella orders twelve trout.

Brin is no saboteur. Charge the joke to him. He returns fish, carries the guest slate, and confirms each river guest's actual needs. Under Pella's eye he becomes a surprisingly good interviewer, because nobody wants him answering on their behalf.

If confronted cruelly, Brin retreats into mockery. If given accountable work, he admits, “I thought the lie was too decorated to stand.” Pella answers, “Then next time decorate the truth and carry the basket.”

Brin can later serve as a useful master of abstention tokens or children’s lanterns. His comedy should reveal the danger of assumptions without making river custom itself ridiculous.

## Market complications

For pressure, choose one or two complications rooted in appetite, labor, or a bad inference. Do not import whimsy from nowhere.

1. **The leaking trout tank.** Water loss, crowding, and the cracked cart wheel force immediate action before discussion.
2. **Nine uncounted companions.** The family lists include people differently. The witnesses must confirm invitations instead of announcing a universal counting rule.
3. **The cold onion cakes.** A ceremonial meeting overruns the oven schedule. Pella asks which course changes, who eats later, or who leaves the meeting to help.
4. **The honorable wrong bed.** Deep-Reed is assigned a prestigious willow niche she cannot reach, while Noll Ferry receives a damp riverside cot he cannot safely use.
5. **The head-seat dispute.** Aunt Sal and a river duty-holder both expect the same sight line for different reasons. Rebuilding the table or clarifying function solves more than declaring rank.
6. **The surprise cradle ribbon.** Aunt Sal plans to tie it around Neris during the feast. Neris may accept another use, receive it privately, or refuse it. Stop the surprise before a crowd can turn refusal into spectacle.
7. **The borrowed cups.** Half the cups belong to people who must leave before cleanup. Reassigning washing labor becomes a test of whether gratitude includes practical return.

## Productive scenes

- The party returns the protected trout through a crowded market while keeping them alive.
- Pella makes each witness replace a category on the guest list with three named people and their actual needs.
- Jory refuses to become an anonymous “safe substitute” and negotiates public credit and extra hands.
- Deep-Reed and Noll Ferry exchange their assigned lodgings and expose why the original sorting failed.
- Aunt Sal bargains for recognition by accepting cup collection or guest guidance as a real office.
- Brin confirms preferences under Pella’s eye and discovers that accurate answers can be funnier than inventions.
- A trial gift declaration lets Agnes, Orren, Mara, or Jory hear how the receiver understands the object.
- Rain demonstrates the bounded grievance procedure with the broken market-basket debt she owes Agnes.
- Pella lays out recoverable, perishable, committed, and lost stores so postponement can be discussed honestly.

## Decisions that travel forward

Record at least the following before leaving the market:

- confirmed and uncertain guest counts;
- named access and lodging assignments;
- menu courses, sources, abstentions, and labor;
- table form and the seats whose placement serves a specific function;
- the grievance stewards, submission deadline, public limit, and later hearings;
- each gift’s status: accepted, transformed, private, deferred, or refused;
- unresolved work and the person who must answer it; and
- recoverable versus irrecoverable costs if the wedding is postponed.

These decisions should become visible at the rehearsal and wedding. A guest who was not confirmed may arrive late or stay away. A promised handrail must exist. A source should be named when the dish is served. An abstention token should remain visible. A transformed gift should carry both the giver’s intention and the receiver’s stated limit.

## If the market is skipped or reached late

A wedding can proceed without a full feast. It cannot honestly pretend that unasked guests were accommodated or that uncertain food was responsibly sourced. Use only stores whose source, labor, and safety are known. Reduce the guest-facing meal before guessing at appetites. A simple bread, orchard, cheese, and sea-catch table is better than a symbolic menu built from protected trout or unnamed labor.

Undeclared gifts remain private or withheld. The public grievance interval occurs only if speakers, respondents, duties, stewards, and later hearings were prepared. Otherwise, Pella begins the meal at the fixed cue and directs unfinished obligations to private hearings. Nobody is surprised into public testimony.

Late market corrections reduce scale rather than rewrite consent. A guest may change attendance, seating, lodging, food, gift, dance, or grievance participation. Update that named person's entry. Do not treat one river guest's answer as representative of the river household or one Vale relative's answer as binding the mill table.

## Failure forward

The feast may become smaller, split, delayed, or less symmetrical. Some guests may lodge apart. A course may be omitted. A gift may be withheld. A grievance may move to private hearing. A guest may remain present while abstaining. None of these outcomes invalidates the marriage.

Hospitality goes false when it keeps speaking in collective nouns after particular people have answered. Carry the damage forward at human scale: cold food, an inaccessible seat, an offended giver, unpaid labor, a missing guest, or a public correction at the wedding. The repair is the same each time—name the person, the authority, and the revisable promise.
