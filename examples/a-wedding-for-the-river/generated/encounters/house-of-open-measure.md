# The House of Open Measure

**Role:** necessary, start  
**Tags:** briefing, witnesses, consent, compact

Two identical compacts lie beneath two river stones. Their three sentences place Tomas and Neris in different homes, offices, names, and lines of care.

## Opening View

Mera Quill waits until the alder leaf has passed beneath the table before she speaks. “These are the same words.” Two wedding compacts lie under smooth river stones, identical down to the last stroke; the notes in their margins would make two different marriages. Four ledgers remain closed beside them. The folio meant to hold the witnesses’ account is blank. Mera keeps her hands behind her back, as though even touching the page might answer a question that is not hers.

## Encounter Material

## Situation
The **House of Open Measure** stands where alder shade gives way to the mill channel: a square, whitewashed room with two doors and no privileged entrance. Dry benches face a public copying table. Scales hang on one wall. A shallow runnel comes through the river door, passes beneath the table, and leaves by the road.

The adventurers are the **free witnesses**. The regular circuit is trapped at an upstream boundary hearing, and the three-day witness period has begun without them. Mera may receive filings, preserve the paired record, advise on form, and draft the folio. Because she accepted the readings and will write the folio, she may not also serve as the independent field witness who certifies it. The adventurers might belong to the traveling circuit, carry its seal after earlier service, or simply be the nearest credible outsiders. Their qualification is mostly absence: no kin here, no water lease, no inheritance, no household debt, no share in the old flood quarrel.

Mera Quill gives them four duties:

1. ask whether **Tomas Vale** and **Neris Reed-in-Rain** choose this marriage now;
2. find the customs, gifts, words, and public acts that do different work on opposite banks;
3. write the meaning the principals accept, with its bearer, limit, refusal, and ending; and
4. sign only those acts they observe or later verify.

The seal opens doors and earns answers. It confers no judgment over love, household, mill, river, or marriage. The witnesses may call a pause. They may not fill it.

## The contradiction on the table

**Mera Quill**, the resident recorder, has placed two copies of the compact side by side. Six weeks ago, each household lodged a sealed reading copy with her. The shared ceremonial lines were known; the margins explaining how each household expected those lines to operate were not. Concord practice opens the copies together at the beginning of the witness period so neither side can revise its gloss after seeing the other's. The protection worked. The disagreement is visible and attributable—but late.

The copied text is identical:

> Tomas enters the house of Neris. Neris keeps the wheel. Each bears the other's name before water and flame.

The mortal Vale household reads the first sentence as ceremonial courtesy before Neris enters Tomas's hearth. The river household reads it as Tomas's adoption into Neris's current. The Vales read “keeps the wheel” as Neris becoming mistress of the mill. The river kin read it as a promise that the mill wheel will remain answerable to the river and never become the bride's private property. “Bears the other's name” is equally unstable: mortal records expect a fixed household name, while river bynames change with season, deed, grief, and residence.

Nobody forged the document. Nobody hid a clause. Tomas and Neris signed a notice of intended marriage and agreed on many ordinary arrangements; they did not approve either household's marginal claims. They asked their elders to choose the ceremonial formula because neither wanted marriage to look like a rejection of the household that had formed them, especially after Perrin Vale's death. Each assumed their practical agreement would govern any grand words. That was a shared evasion, not a trap.

The same flood made the old formula attractive to both sides. The Vales hear Neris entering their hearth, keeping the wheel, and preserving Perrin's place among the household dead. The river kin hear Tomas entering a current of reciprocal duty and making the wheel answer warnings that once died at its threshold. One sentence appears to mend the same wound in opposite directions.

Mera will not send forward for certification a compact whose words can place two people in different households at the same instant. The wedding is three evenings away, when the lowering sun passes through the old wheel and lays a bright path from the mill yard to the willow bank. The date is not an apocalyptic deadline. The feast can be eaten, guests can go home, and the couple can marry next year. But postponement would publicly harden every present doubt: that Tomas is surrendering the mill, that Neris is being captured on dry land, or that their affection could not survive plain speech.

## The couple are not one position

Tomas and Neris want the wedding. They do not want precisely the same marriage, and neither has yet found language that keeps the other from paying for the difference.

Tomas answers pressure by fixing the nearest thing. At Mera's table he calls the eastern rooms “our home” and the willow house “her other home,” then stops when Neris repeats the possessives. He will accept exact warning, closure, rescue, and hearing duties. He will not exchange Vale belonging for river belonging or let a generous promise quietly assign Mara and the mill workers.

Neris corrects him without withdrawing from the rooms they have prepared. She expects to sleep there most ordinary nights and beneath the willows when current work requires it. What she refuses is the idea that one dwelling becomes real while the other survives by permission. She will not spend the marriage translating every river warning, relative, and claim through herself.

Their keys and schedules already fit better than their descriptions. Neither wants marriage or adoption to move the mill, the water lease, or inherited obligations. The live disagreement is whether they are one household in two dwellings or two continuing households joined by marriage. That difference reaches warning duties, care, names, future claims, and who may speak when one principal is absent. The witnesses must make those consequences explicit without choosing the description for them.

## Mera Quill
Ink has worked permanently into the side of Mera's left hand, but she keeps that hand off the blank folio while the witnesses speak. The grander a compromise grows, the quieter she becomes. Then she turns the page sideways and asks:

- Who acts?
- Who may refuse?
- What event calls the duty?
- What ends it?
- How will the person carrying flour tomorrow say this without changing it?

She will compare precedents and draft more than one honest form. She will not announce a best answer. If the witnesses settle everything with the families and return for a ceremonial yes, Mera closes the folio, reties the red cord, and says, “You have recorded everybody except the people marrying. Begin again.”

## The private consent procedure

The House uses a four-pass procedure whenever household interests may make public agreement easier than honest disagreement. Run it at least once during the first day and again, briefly, before the ceremony.

### 1. Separate desire

Question Tomas and Neris in different rooms, beyond relatives and gifts. Ask whether each wishes to marry **now**, not merely whether each loves the other or once promised to marry. Either may request postponement without giving the other household a public reason. The witnesses record only “free,” “conditional,” “not yet,” or “refused” unless the speaker asks for more detail to be shared.

### 2. Separate terms

For each unsettled subject—residence, adoption, mill work, water duty, names, care, children, death, and postponement—ask for four answers:

- what the speaker positively wants;
- what the speaker can accept;
- what the speaker will not accept; and
- what the speaker does not yet understand well enough to promise.

Silence, embarrassment, deference, and “whatever Neris wants” or “whatever keeps the Vales easy” are not consent. They are prompts for a more exact question.

### 3. Reciprocal paraphrase

Bring the couple together. Each must describe one material obligation the other is accepting and one limit the other is retaining. Perfect legal language is unnecessary. A paraphrase fails only when the listener says, “That is not what I meant,” or when it leaves out the person who must bear the cost.

### 4. Repeated choice

After the witnesses draft possible language, question the couple again. A private answer becomes part of the compact only when its speaker repeats or adopts it in the other's presence. Changing an answer is evidence that the procedure worked, not evidence of unreliability.

Compelled speech, read thoughts, and truth-punishing magic answer the wrong question. The House needs a choice the speaker understands and may revise, not metaphysical proof of sincerity.

## Their first separate answers

These answers establish live tensions. They are starting positions, not predetermined solutions.

| Subject | Tomas | Neris |
|---|---|---|
| Marriage now | Yes, if Neris is not being asked to become the mill's living lease | Yes, if river adoption is a real obligation rather than ceremonial decoration |
| Ordinary residence | The eastern rooms are their home; duty may take either elsewhere | The eastern rooms and willow house are both homes; neither requires leave from the other |
| Mill standing | Neris may share management by agreement but receives no ownership or inherited debt by marriage | She wants authority over household matters and water compliance, not title to machinery |
| River adoption | He accepts defined warning, evacuation, spawning, rescue, and hearing duties | He must be answerable beyond the wheel gate, but not generally commandable by her kin |
| Names | He remains Tomas Vale and will accept a river byname only if later earned | She keeps an acknowledged chain of river names and will use “of Vale Mill” as an address, not a replacement surname |
| Future children | No household should claim them by default; he wants them secure in the mortal registry | Both households must owe care; household standing should be chosen later, not fixed at birth |
| Incapacity or death | The surviving partner keeps the eastern rooms and may call on both households for care | Neither family may remove an incapacitated spouse without that person's prior direction or the other's agreement |
| Postponement | Painful but preferable to a promise Neris cannot safely paraphrase | Acceptable if one of them needs it; she will not permit the families to call delay abandonment |

Do not copy this table into a contract. Test every answer at the mill, beneath the willows, and against an ordinary week.

## Terms that can become clauses

The following modules are available once the party has learned enough to propose them. They are not a canonical settlement; Tomas and Neris may combine, revise, or reject them.

### Household and residence

- The eastern rooms are the couple's shared dwelling at the mill.
- The willow house remains Neris's household standing and a second dwelling available to both partners.
- Neither partner requires permission to answer an existing duty at another dwelling.
- An absence does not become abandonment unless the absent partner refuses an agreed request for contact or hearing.

The couple must still decide whether the record calls this **one household in two dwellings** or **two households joined by marriage**. The practical duties can be identical while the recognition differs.

### Mill and water

- Mill buildings, machinery, debts, wages, and mortal succession remain under the Vale deed and account books.
- Water diversion, spawning closure, drought restriction, and flood-warning duties remain under the Concord lease.
- Neris may act as the mill household's named water correspondent without becoming owner or debtor.
- Tomas may not invoke marriage to speak for the river household beyond duties it explicitly assigns him.

### Adoption duties

Tomas may accept a bounded set of duties:

1. answer one of three recognized warning signals;
2. stop the wheel and open the lower sluice when the agreed flood threshold is met;
3. ring the mortal bell and help clear workers, tenants, and animals from the yard;
4. honor posted spawning closures and prevent mill employees from evading them;
5. carry or relay downstream warnings when able;
6. accept rescue and appear at a mixed hearing before using force in a water dispute.

Adoption does not transfer property, cancel Vale kinship, impose unrestricted labor, require Tomas to enter dangerous water beyond his ability, or let one household conceal a warning from the other.

### Names

A stable record may read:

> Neris, child of Serein-at-Alderfall, entered in the House index under her unchanging witness mark, presently answering to Reed-in-Rain and dwelling also at Vale Mill.

Later bynames append to the chain rather than overwrite it. “Neris of Vale Mill” may describe residence. “Neris Vale” is used only if she freely chooses it, which she presently does not. Tomas remains Tomas Vale. No river byname is invented for the wedding; one may be recorded if later earned and accepted.

### Care and future children

- Each partner may call on either household for urgent care.
- Neither household gains default custody of an ill, injured, or grieving spouse.
- A future child is entered in the mortal birth register and the river care ledger without either entry deciding permanent household standing.
- Named adults from both households owe food, rescue, shelter, and notice.
- No immersion, apprenticeship, change of name, removal from a dwelling, or assignment of inheritance occurs merely because one household claims custom.
- Household standing may be revisited when the child can understand the choice; property succession remains governed by the relevant deed rather than by river adoption.

Name the adult who accepts each care duty. “Both families will help” is not a clause.

## Immediate problems

Before the witnesses can finish reading, three preparations arrive at once.

- **Agnes Vale**, Tomas's grandmother, brings her wedding veil and announces that Neris will wear it.
- **Orren Underbridge**, Neris's elder brother, brings a polished iron ring and asks whether the dry folk truly intend to close metal around his sister.
- A market runner reports that the feast committee has ordered twelve river trout from the protected spawning reach because “river guests must want river fish.”

Each problem is sincere, visible, and reversible. No lore check supplies the correct culture. Learn what the giver meant, what the receiver hears, and whether another use can preserve the gift without preserving the injury.

## Starting movement

Mera recommends that the party divide their attention between the two households before the market closes. Tomas can guide them through the mill. Neris can bring them safely beneath the willows. The compact remains at the House under paired stones, and either family may add proposed language in a separate margin; nobody may alter the common text without both betrothed present.

The House remains available for comparison, private consent checks, and revised drafts. A return should clarify the record, never solve it by authority.

## Running the commission out of order

Mera draws four narrow columns beside the compact: **observed**, **proposed**, **accepted**, and **contested**. She keeps two smaller marks for **refused** and **unasked**. These are not grades. They prevent a useful idea from becoming a promise merely because several people discussed it.

After the opening contradiction is clear, all six preparation sites are available. The market, shrine, yard, bridge, mill, and willow house are ordinary places with known roads and expected hours. The clue graph records where the witnesses can learn useful conclusions and corroborate them; it does not create locked doors. The rehearsal and wedding are scheduled in advance. A party can arrive at either with unfinished work.

If the witnesses divide, each group may observe, ask questions, reserve reversible resources, draft wording, and carry offers. A group cannot accept for an absent principal or household officer. On reunion, each group answers four questions:

1. What did you directly observe?
2. What did you propose?
3. Who accepted the exact proposal, and within what authority?
4. What remains refused, conditional, contested, or unasked?

Mera records where Tomas, Neris, and traveling principals actually are. A named person does not appear in two simultaneous scenes because a dramatic answer would be convenient. Messages take the time and route the table has established. A witness who did not observe a private answer may later verify it only through explicit repetition, not through another witness's confidence.

The House remains open for private questions and revised drafts, but returning after every encounter is unnecessary. Bring the compact back when a term requires reciprocal paraphrase, a speaker requests privacy, authority is disputed, or the witnesses need to compare incompatible records. Otherwise, the common ledger can travel with paired witness marks and be reconciled at the next actual meeting.

## One-sided cooperation

The House separates six matters that families often compress into one word: personal marriage, Vale household standing, river household standing, river adoption, mill authority, and House certification. Each may have a different answer.

If the Vale household cooperates and the river household does not, Tomas and Neris may still marry as individuals. Neris retains her existing river standing, but Tomas is not adopted and the river household owes no unaccepted reciprocal duties. If the river household cooperates and the Vales do not, Tomas may accept exact river duties, but Neris gains no Vale property, office, debt, succession, or household recognition by implication. If both households withhold recognition, the couple may still exchange personal promises and depart together; the House may witness that ceremony while declining cross-bank household certification.

Internal division is ordinary. Agnes may lend the veil while Mara refuses an operational clause. Orren may give an open ring while Serein defers adoption. Record people and offices separately. Nobody may turn "the family" into an imaginary unanimous speaker.

A household officer may withhold only what belongs to that office: property, labor, recognition, reciprocal duty, participation, testimony, or signature. Such a refusal changes the ceremony state. It does not answer whether Tomas and Neris marry. The witnesses similarly control only the scope of their own certification.

## Failure forward

A failed consent scene does not reveal that the relationship was secretly false. It exposes a term that is not ready. Mark it as conditional, return it to the relevant encounter, or leave it for a later hearing. If one partner requests postponement, the House protects that choice without requiring an accusation. If the couple proceeds without resolving a clause, Mera can record the exact boundary. If one partner cannot freely answer “yes” at the final repetition, the witnesses cannot certify the wedding even if affection and every preparation remain intact.

## The hospitality appendix

Mera’s first guest sheet has two broad columns: “Vale” and “river.” Both are crossed out. The replacement register names each person and separates ceremony attendance, feast attendance, lodging, food, access, abstention, gift, and grievance roles.

Mera explains that household affiliation is useful for invitation authority but almost useless for accommodation. The House will record who confirmed each entry and whether a guest’s presence extends to a particular rite. Attendance at the wedding does not imply participation in every course, grievance, gift, or dance.

The register keeps the couple from becoming universal translators. Pella, Mera, hosts, and stewards carry named responsibilities. Neris does not spend her wedding explaining every river guest; Tomas does not answer for every Vale relative because he knows their names.
