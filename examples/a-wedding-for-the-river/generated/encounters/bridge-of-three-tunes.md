# The Bridge of Three Tunes

**Role:** necessary  
**Tags:** music, dance, grievances, rehearsal

On boards that magnify every missed step, Iven and Moss teach four and six to meet without making either tune background—and mark the silences no music may cross.

## Opening View

Four steps. Six beats. At twelve, the wedding collides with itself. Tomas reaches the landing precisely where Neris was told to cross, and the dancers freeze with raised hands. The bridge is narrow enough to make an instrument of every heel, cane, wheel, and dropped cup. Iven’s fiddle falls silent. Moss lowers his pipe. Beneath them, the river keeps a measure neither musician wrote, and for a few breaths no one is willing to count over it.

## Encounter Material

## Situation
The **Bridge of Three Tunes** is narrow enough that every heel, cane, chair wheel, and dropped cup enters the music. Rehearsals happen here because soft ground cannot hide a missed joining point. Today the bridge holds two tunes, three arguments, eight dancers, Deep-Reed's basin chair, and a child who believes tempo is a moral virtue.

**Iven Crossbow** has written the Mill Road in square four-step phrases. **Moss-in-Evening** has shaped the Reed Turn in six, lengthening the last breath before it returns. Each can follow the other. Neither will call following equal leadership when one tune remains recognizable and the other becomes atmosphere.

Decide whose movement each tune serves, where leadership changes, how four and six meet after twelve pulses, what non-dancers do, and how the score travels to meadow, yard, willow bank, or shrine. Mark the silences. No downbeat may carry consent, correction, names, gifts, vows, grievances, or the couple's first shared step past the person who owns it.

Music gives a crowd a first step and a memory of the route. It must not give Tomas or Neris an answer.

## Iven Crossbow
Iven is a mill-town fiddler, dance caller, and former levy signaler. His byname comes from the level way he holds the bow while somebody else argues. He thinks in entries, repeated phrases, downbeats, and the practical need for a crowd to know when to move.

He wants the Mill Road to remain a tune, not a quotation before river music takes the room. He also wants Agnes to know when to stand, Pella when to serve, and the couple when to move without waiting in public confusion.

Iven will change repetitions, yield first or last lead, hold silence, adapt to canes and chairs, write separate routes, or abandon formal dance. He will not play beneath consent, accept “something mortal” as a specification, call an inaudible pulse equal leadership, or learn of a site move after the guests begin walking.

Iven speaks in entries and endings:

> “Wonder can move a crowd. It still needs a first step.”

> “If I am background, say background and pay background. Do not call it joining.”

> “Silence is a measure if everyone knows who holds it.”

## Moss-in-Evening
Moss is a reed-piper, current-listener, and keeper of work songs used when several boats enter one narrow reach. They watch bodies and water more than paper. Their phrases turn, delay, and arrive from another side without losing the bank.

Moss wants river guests to hear change as intention. Counting is not the problem. A count becomes a problem when every extension is called lateness and every circular path is treated as failure to proceed.

They will accept fixed joining points, cue maps, a repeated foot pulse, shorter phrases, spoken calls, separate approaches, seated listeners, or an unaccompanied departure. They will not hold one long note beneath the fiddle, force dancers into square files for a clean picture, play under grievances, or let improvisation rewrite an agreed act.

Moss answers in turns rather than conclusions:

> “The turn is not where the tune gets lost. The turn is how it knows the bank.”

> “You keep asking which beat is first. Ask who is moving.”

> “I can follow him. That is not the same as disappearing behind him.”

## The three tunes

The bridge gives its name to a practical three-part score. The couple may use all three, two, or none.

### The Mill Road

Iven’s four-step tune supports direct walking, chairs, canes, and people carrying objects. Its phrase is easy to repeat and easy to stop. Mortal guests recognize it as an entrance tune, but it need not belong exclusively to them. Anyone may walk it.

Count it as four equal foot pulses:

1. step or prepare;
2. step;
3. step;
4. arrive or turn.

The fourth pulse is not automatically a conclusion. It can open space for Moss’s response, an announcement, or silence.

### The Reed Turn

Moss’s six-step tune supports circling, side movement, and approaches that adjust around bodies or water. The final pulse may lengthen for breath without changing the next joining point.

Count it as:

1. move;
2. answer;
3. move;
4. turn;
5. return;
6. open.

These words teach the count; they are not sacred terms. River dancers may move differently. What matters is the opening at the end, not a hard stop.

### The Joining Measure

Four and six meet every twelve pulses. Dessa’s crews already peg panels in four while passing lashings in six; the market fountain rounds use the same coincidence. The joining measure can therefore feel locally familiar rather than mathematically imposed.

At pulses four and eight, Iven’s phrase arrives while Moss continues. At pulse six, Moss opens while Iven is midway through a phrase. At pulse twelve, both arrive together. The score can use that structure in several ways:

- each tradition leads a complete phrase before the shared arrival;
- Tomas walks four while Neris turns six, meeting at twelve;
- Iven plays the first four, Moss the next six, and both share the final two pulses;
- a repeated four-step foot pulse continues while leadership alternates in the melody; or
- the musicians remain separate until one unmistakable twelve-pulse joining point.

Do not require the players to perform arithmetic at the table. Let Iven tap four, Moss tap six, and have everyone hear the twelfth pulse arrive together. The scene asks who leads and what movement the pattern serves, not whether the characters pass a music exam.

## Leadership, answer, and joining

A score is complete only when each section names its leader, answer, and visible action. “Both play together” is too vague.

### Alternating leadership

Iven leads the Vale approach. Moss answers with the river approach. At the central threshold, Moss leads the adoption turn and Iven answers. Both lead the departure phrase.

This is legible and honors distinct offices. It can become overly segmented if every act is divided into equal cultural portions regardless of meaning.

### Simultaneous approaches

Tomas and Neris approach from different routes while both tunes sound. The melodies meet at the consent station, stop entirely for consent, then resume in a new shared pattern.

This makes the joining dramatic but demands space, rehearsal, and clear sight lines. One delayed route can leave a person waiting publicly.

### Shared pulse, changing melody

A quiet foot pulse supports accessible movement while fiddle and pipe trade melodic leadership. This lets guests with canes or chairs know when to move. It risks making Iven’s contribution structurally necessary but artistically invisible. If used, the pulse should receive explicit recognition as a leading office rather than being described as mere accompaniment.

### Separate tunes, one answer

Each household hears its full tune before the ceremony. The only combined music is a short response after the promises and a shared departure. This works well if rehearsal time is short or families resist a blended score. It preserves difference without forcing synthesis into every minute.

### No processional dance

The couple and guests move in ordinary walking silence. The musicians play only at gathering, exchange, and departure. This may be the most inclusive form. Present it as chosen, not as what remains after music fails.

The couple chooses among these structures. Iven and Moss may explain craft consequences, but musical pride does not outrank Tomas’s and Neris’s desired public movement.

## The six cues

The shrine’s six ceremonial functions become six score cues. Iven marks them with square notches; Moss marks them with curved cuts. The final score should be readable even if one musician is absent.

### Consent cue

All music stops before the public consent questions. The stop is long enough that no lingering note appears to answer for the couple. If either answer changes, Ansel raises an open hand and the musicians remain silent until Mera or the couple gives a new cue.

### Household-standing cue

The music identifies entry and reciprocity. If Tomas uses the boat, Moss may lead his step into it, Iven may sound the retained Vale response, and both may mark the exit. If Neris crosses beneath the veil canopy, the Vale tune can lead without requiring her to take a surname or fixed course.

### Name cue

The announcer speaks over silence. After each chosen name, the musician associated with the person’s present household offers a short answer; the other musician then answers the retained or joined standing. If Neris authorizes no congregational repetition, the instruments can acknowledge the name without guests attempting it.

### Exchange cue

Each giver receives a brief approach phrase. The receiver’s acceptance, transformation, deferral, or refusal occurs in silence. Music resumes only after the receiver has spoken. A withheld object may receive no music or a neutral closing phrase, according to the receiver’s choice.

### Promise cue

No continuous underscoring. Music can introduce a personal vow and answer after it, but the duty-bearing words remain unmasked. In a compact-first order, Mera’s summary is silent and the personal promises receive separate short frames.

### Departure cue

The joining measure begins only after both partners have completed the final function. The first shared step is theirs. Musicians and guests follow. The departure tune should end at a place the couple can actually occupy together rather than at a picturesque split in the path.

These cues let the score survive changes in props. Ring, veil, boat, and handstone may be present, transformed, private, or absent while the musical functions remain legible.

## Participation without compulsory dance

The guest register should determine movement options by person.

- Agnes may remain seated and tap one finger against the chair arm if she wishes.
- Deep-Reed can participate through foot movement in her basin without leaving the backed chair.
- Seat Noll Ferry near open air, outside the turning dancers.
- Aunt Sal wants to take the wet handrail route and may lead a short mortal response if asked privately.
- Children may carry lantern boats in a separate round after the certified ceremony rather than weaving through vow stations.
- Guests using abstention tokens may listen without clapping, singing, dancing, or repeating a response.

Presence at a musical act does not imply assent to its custom. The announcer can say, “Those who wish may answer,” and the score can leave enough space for silence to remain visible.

If the couple wants a communal dance, hold it after the six functions and after the witnesses have made or withheld certification. That prevents social pressure on the dance floor from becoming part of legal consent.

## Grievance and music

The bounded grievance interval belongs before shared food, not necessarily inside the wedding ceremony. If the couple includes one public obligation at the ceremony, the music must clarify scope rather than heighten accusation.

Use a bounded form:

1. Rain names the grievance procedure in silence.
2. The speaker states one duty and requested next act.
3. The respondent answers or accepts later hearing.
4. The steward places the obligation stone in the bowl.
5. Moss plays one open phrase; Iven answers with one closed phrase; neither implies resolution.
6. The ceremony proceeds.

Do not play beneath the words. Do not repeat a family theme to make one speaker appear villainous. Do not use a triumphant joining measure when a grievance is merely carried forward. Music can mark that an obligation has entered stewardship without pretending the parties agree.

The bridge is a good place to rehearse Agnes’s basket debt with Rain because the issue is small, acknowledged, and bounded. It teaches the musicians the difference between open obligation and public quarrel before the old flood appears at rehearsal.

## Movable joining points

Iven and Moss must be able to carry the same structure to four sites.

### Flood meadow

The joining point is the central threshold between paired approaches. Sound carries broadly but can disperse in wind. Use larger gestures and fewer quiet cues.

### Old wheel yard

The joining point is beside the stone runnel after the wheel is stopped. The yard amplifies fiddle and can swallow low pipe notes in residual machinery noise. Move Moss to the runnel edge or use the audible spillway Dessa designed.

### Willow bank

The joining point is the boat landing or broad dry platform. Water supports the pipe but softens foot pulses. Iven may need a board or drum heel to keep movement cues audible.

### Shrine rise

The joining point is the open threshold. The roof strengthens spoken words and fiddle resonance. Keep the pipe outside or near the runnel so its answer does not overwhelm Ansel’s questions.

Rehearse the same twelve-pulse joining at two sites. The characters should notice what changes: travel time, echo, line of sight, volume, and who can move. The leadership pattern should remain intact even if instrumentation or distance changes.

## The rehearsal problem

Begin the main scene with Tomas and Neris attempting simultaneous approaches.

Iven gives Tomas a four-step entry. Moss gives Neris a six-step turn. Both expect the other to adjust at the midpoint. Tomas reaches the landing early, stops, and tries to be polite. Neris completes her phrase, finds him blocking the turn, and steps around him. The dancers behind them collide gently. A child declares that the river has won because it used more steps.

Nobody is incompetent. The instructions lacked a named joining point and did not say who yields space before pulse twelve.

Let the party diagnose the problem physically. They can:

- mark the twelfth pulse on the bridge;
- change starting distances;
- let one person begin later;
- choose alternating rather than simultaneous approaches;
- widen the turn at Dessa’s yard;
- eliminate the processional and retain the joining tune for departure; or
- have Tomas and Neris begin together and give the household tunes to family offices instead.

The best solution is the one the couple prefers and the site can support, not the most sophisticated score.

## Musical complications

1. **The invisible leader.** Iven supplies the pulse everyone follows, but Moss receives all praise for the audible melody. Decide whether to make the pulse explicit, alternate structural leadership, or change the score.
2. **The endless river ending.** Moss’s final phrase keeps opening while Pella needs a firm feast cue. Add a named closing answer rather than demanding that the entire river tune become square.
3. **The loud bridge.** Chairs and canes create stronger percussion than expected. Incorporate it, move listeners, or choose a different route.
4. **The borrowed tune.** Brin teaches children a false “ancient” response. It is musically charming and historically invented. The couple may adopt it as new, reject it, or credit Brin’s authorship; nobody should call it ancestral.
5. **The wrong household theme.** An announcer assumes Neris should enter to the Vale tune after marriage. Neris decides which tune accompanies which act; marriage has not already occurred at her entrance.
6. **The missing musician.** A minor injury or work obligation removes one player from a rehearsal. The written cue map and guest pulse should preserve the sequence without inventing catastrophe.
7. **The forced dance.** A well-meaning relative pulls an abstaining guest into the round. Stop, restore the guest’s choice, and clarify that participation is invitational.

## If the bridge is skipped or reached late

Use spoken cues and silence. The principals approach or remain in place by their own choice, not because music moves them. One musician may accompany after a function, but accompaniment is not evidence that two traditions shared leadership. A ceremony without processional music remains complete.

If only one tradition has rehearsed, do not call simultaneous sound a joining measure. Let that musician lead an acknowledged piece, let the other household answer in speech or silence, and keep the first shared departure step unscored until Tomas and Neris choose it.

A late musical discovery changes performance, not consent already given. Stop a cue that moves the couple too early, makes a required silence impossible, or assigns unwilling guests a response. Replace it with a spoken marker, optional unison, or no music. Iven and Moss may withhold their own leadership; neither controls whether the couple marries.

## Failure forward

The procession may split. The joining point may arrive badly. One tune may dominate, a guest may miss a cue, or the couple may abandon formal dance. Carry the result into the wedding as memory, embarrassment, simplification, or visible asymmetry—not as invalid consent.

The score fails ethically only if it answers for Tomas or Neris, forces participation, hides duty-bearing words, or calls one musician’s disappearance a union. The remedy can be silence, separate tunes, one explicit joining phrase, or ordinary walking. Music should make chosen relations audible. It need not solve them.
