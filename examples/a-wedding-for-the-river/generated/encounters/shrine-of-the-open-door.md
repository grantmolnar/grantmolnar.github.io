# The Doorless Shrine

**Role:** necessary  
**Tags:** vows, records, officiant, consent

Keeper Ansel clears the rite down to six acts, then tests every vow, name, exchange, and open clause in words Tomas and Neris can repeat.

## Opening View

Keeper Ansel has removed almost everything people call a wedding. No altar, no procession, no place of honor: only six wooden blocks laid across the bare floor—consent, household, names, exchange, promises, departure. Fourteen old compacts cover the benches, including one that ends in refusal. On a side shelf, a veil, a ring, and other returned gifts wait without shame. Ansel touches none of them. “Begin,” he says, “with what each person may still decline.”

## Encounter Material

## Situation
The Doorless Shrine has no wall on its river side and no door on its road side. Fourteen compact tablets cover the benches: marriages, adoptions, burial agreements, ferry partnerships, one refusal, and several settlements whose open clauses outlived the people who first signed them. Keeper Ansel Rook has removed the ceremonial objects from the floor and set down six plain wooden blocks.

Each block names an act the witnesses may later certify: **consent, household standing, names, exchange, promises, departure**. Everything else must earn its place by helping Tomas and Neris perform one of those acts without changing it behind their backs. A veil may shelter or mourn. A ring may promise or bind. A boat may receive or remove. A crowd response may recognize a name or manufacture one. Ansel will not let inherited beauty answer those questions by momentum.

The work here is spoken and repeatable. Tomas and Neris choose the order, the words, the silences, and any reserved matter. Each poetic promise must survive an ordinary paraphrase. Each name must identify the person without forcing symmetry. Each object must be offered with a declared office and accepted in that office. If one block cannot yet be crossed, red cord marks the open line; the rest of the ceremony need not pretend the cord is absent.

## Keeper Ansel Rook
Ansel is narrow and weathered, with a voice that carries without force. He keeps compact tablets, marries across customs, and buries strangers whose households dispute the body. Any sentence that wins applause before explanation makes him wary.

He speaks in verbs: enter, retain, answer, warn, care, return, leave, revise. Metaphor is welcome after its duty survives plain speech. Ansel will rearrange the six acts, use one vow or many, keep parallel household descriptions, link stable identity to changing names, transform or omit gifts, mark an open clause, move the site, or let another officiate.

He will not accept a relative's answer for a principal, a name invented for symmetry, a future child assigned as ceremonial property, a surprise duty, compelled truth, or a record strengthened by fatigue and silence.

Ansel's sentences are plain enough to remember:

> “A vow may be beautiful after it has survived being ordinary.”

> “Do not promise forever until you can tell me what you owe next winter.”

> “The record is allowed an open door. That is what doors are for.”

## The six ceremonial functions

The six markers are an audit, not six mandatory speeches. Several functions may share one act; one function may require several. A sequence is ready when the table can say what an observer sees and what Tomas and Neris say it means.

### 1. Consent

The ceremony needs a public choice that follows, rather than replaces, the House of Open Measure’s private consent procedure. Ansel asks each person three short questions:

1. Do you choose this marriage now?
2. Do you choose the duties entered in the witness record?
3. Is any stated matter provisional, refused, or reserved for later hearing?

Tomas and Neris answer separately, without completing one another’s sentences. They need not disclose private reasons. “Not yet” is a valid answer. “Yes, except for the child-standing clause reserved to later hearing” is more certifiable than an exhausted unqualified yes.

If either person changes an answer from the House interviews, pause. Mera records the change; Ansel asks whether new language is needed; the other partner paraphrases the revised term; and the ceremony resumes, moves the disputed function later, or postpones. Guests may be disappointed. Disappointment is not coercive authority.

### 2. Household standing

The public act must show what is entered, retained, or created without pretending that household has one uncontested definition. Ansel offers three workable forms.

- **One household, two dwellings.** Tomas and Neris name the eastern rooms and the willow house as homes of one marriage, then state the duties that apply in each place.
- **Two continuing households joined.** Each retains standing in their present household and accepts defined duties toward the other, while the eastern rooms become a shared dwelling rather than a new corporate household.
- **A new household of the open reach.** The couple names a third marital household whose members retain Vale and river standing. It is elegant only if they want the third standing and accept the extra record work; conciliation alone is no reason to create it.

The ceremony does not need to settle the couple’s preferred description if every consequence is explicit. It does need to state keys, return, warning, care, domestic authority, adoption duties, and retained kinship. Ansel tests any formula by asking, “Who may return at midnight without permission?” and “Who answers a spawning closure when Neris is upriver?”

### 3. Names

The shrine registry uses four separate lines:

- **stable witness identity**, tied to a mark, parentage, and prior records;
- **current answered name**, spoken at the ceremony;
- **recognized bynames**, appended in sequence rather than overwritten;
- **dwellings and household standing**, recorded without turning residence into a surname.

Neris presently chooses **Neris Reed-in-Rain**. Her stable witness identity links her to Serein-at-Alderfall, the House Beneath the Willows, and her earlier child-name, Small-Reed-After-Thunder. A later byname may be appended if she accepts it. The earlier names remain searchable and do not become names guests may use against her preference.

Tomas remains **Tomas Vale**. He may be described as adopted to the House Beneath the Willows if he accepts that standing. He does not need a river byname, and Ansel will not invent one to make the folio appear balanced. If the river household later gives him a byname through ordinary relationship, the House can append it with Tomas’s consent.

A person announcing the couple should use the names they currently answer. The written record may be more extensive than the public announcement. Guests who cannot pronounce a byname can rehearse it, ask permission for an accepted short form, or remain silent at the naming response. Difficulty is not license to replace a name.

### 4. Exchange

An exchange can be an object, an act, a shared use, or no material gift. The minimum procedure is exact:

1. The giver states source, maker, intended honor, and attached duty.
2. The receiver states what they understand themselves to be accepting.
3. The receiver accepts, transforms, defers, receives privately, or refuses.
4. The witnesses state any alteration that changes the object’s public meaning.

The veil might become a canopy under which both stand, a wrapping around the compact chest, or a cloth held behind the couple while the dead are named. The iron ring might become an open spiral, two matching clasps, or a fitting in the threshold bridge. Mara’s handstone might support the shared water bowl or rest on a table that moves between homes. The boat can serve the household-standing function rather than the exchange function. No object must carry more meanings than the people handling it can explain.

Ansel has a basket of blank wooden counters for a useful rehearsal. Each giver places a counter where the object will be used and speaks the intended sentence without displaying the object. Tomas and Neris then paraphrase. If the sentence fails, nobody has yet been pressured by family attention or craft expense.

Beside the counters lies Ansel's return-shelf register. It records a veil accepted as a canopy, a cup returned unopened, and a household token received in private. None of those marriages failed because the exchange changed or stopped. The register keeps three separate lines—what the giver meant, what the receiver accepted, and what finally happened to the object—so later readers cannot turn courtesy into consent.

### 5. Promises

Promises should name continuing duties, limits, revision, and release. Ansel distinguishes four things that “while this river runs” presently collapses:

- **continuing obligation:** a duty owed without seasonal renewal;
- **renewed obligation:** a duty reviewed at a named interval or changed condition;
- **interrupted obligation:** a duty paused by absence, incapacity, danger, or conflicting stewardship;
- **released obligation:** a duty ended through separation, replacement, discharge, or mutual revision.

The couple can choose one of several vow structures.

#### Plain paired vows

Each names the other, the chosen marriage, the dwellings or households recognized, the principal care and warning duties accepted, and the right to reopen terms when circumstances change. This is the clearest form and the easiest for guests to hear.

#### Image followed by plain speech

The poetic line is spoken first, then translated by the speaker. Tomas might say that he will keep faith “while wheel and water answer one another,” then add that he means he will not ignore Neris’s warnings, conceal mill decisions affecting the channel, or treat changed residence as abandonment. Neris might promise “through every honest course,” then state that she means returning, communicating absence, sharing care, and revising duties rather than disappearing into a new current.

#### Separate duty vows

The couple makes shorter promises at the relevant acts: care at the veil or canopy, bounded adoption at the boat, mutual warning at the water bowl, residence at the keys, and endurance at the final joining. This prevents one speech from becoming an unreadable contract but may lengthen the ceremony.

#### Compact affirmation

Mera reads a concise summary of the recorded terms. Each partner affirms that summary and may add one personal promise outside the legal record. This works well in rain or when long public speech would exclude guests, but the summary must still be available for paraphrase beforehand.

None of these structures needs a detailed separation ceremony at the wedding. The record should nevertheless name the right to request revision, the stewards for a future hearing, immediate care and property protections during separation, and the fact that ending a marriage does not make the original choice fraudulent.

### 6. Departure

Departure shows that the couple leaves the ceremony together by present choice. It decides no permanent residence. Stage the route so it does not contradict the household terms.

Possible forms include:

- Tomas enters the threshold boat, Neris receives him into river kinship, and both leave the boat over the shared bridge toward the eastern rooms;
- the couple approaches from separate banks, joins at the runnel, and leaves along the mill path before returning later to the willows;
- the couple begins together beneath the veil canopy, completes the six functions, and departs by the old ford carrying the compact chest between them; or
- in severe rain, they cross the shrine threshold together, touch water and dry stone, and leave through the open side while the musicians carry the joining tune outdoors.

The departure must be possible for the actual bodies, weather, and site. A symbolic route that requires Neris to stand away from moving water too long or Tomas to enter unsafe depth is not more sacred for being difficult.

## The tablet archive

Ansel’s records provide precedents without supplying a hidden correct answer. Choose two or three tablets according to the party’s questions.

### Alder-Sill compact

A ferryman and a carpenter retained separate households for thirty-one years. Their compact named one shared bedroom above the ferry office, two separate property books, reciprocal rescue duties, and an annual winter review. The record calls them “one marriage across two houses” without deciding whether that phrase created a third household. The marriage endured because its practical terms could be revised without relitigating affection.

### Greybank compact

A mill widow promised to receive “all that comes to my spouse’s door.” She meant kin and guests. Her spouse’s road household understood debts, fugitives, and sworn dependents. Both paraphrased the line differently after the ceremony, and neither had been asked beforehand. The marriage did not fail immediately, but the phrase caused years of contested liability. Ansel uses the tablet to show why poetry must be tested against inconvenient examples.

### Reed-and-Candle compact

Two partners agreed on residence, care, names, and exchange but could not agree whether an adopted child would enter one inheritance line. They married with the issue reserved to a mixed hearing after the child’s seventh year, if the child existed and if the affected child wished the question heard. No child was born. The open clause harmed nobody and prevented a hypothetical person from being assigned for the sake of a complete-looking tablet.

### Stonewake refusal

A groom repeated every family-approved answer but could not describe the household duties in private without looking to his elder brother. The witnesses postponed certification. The couple married six months later under shorter terms after the groom had taken separate lodging and negotiated without his brother present. The tablet records the first refusal and the later marriage without shame.

## Vow workshop

Run the workshop as comparison rather than composition by committee.

1. Tomas and Neris each bring one sentence they want to keep.
2. The other partner paraphrases the sentence and names any feared consequence.
3. Ansel supplies one inconvenient case: flood, absence, illness, changed work, separation, or a new byname.
4. The speaker revises, narrows, divides, or keeps the sentence with an explanatory line.
5. Mera marks whether the result is record language, ceremony language, personal promise, or unresolved hope.

Family members may explain what a phrase means in their own custom. They may not negotiate the couple’s answer while either partner is absent. The adventurers can propose alternatives, ask consequences, compare precedents, and protect time for private consultation.

No perfect vow is required. Tomas and Neris must know which words create duties, which express hope, and which hopes remain outside the compact.

## Ceremony orders

Leave the shrine with one primary order and one weather-shortened order.

### The meeting order

- separate approaches;
- public consent;
- names;
- household standing and boat threshold;
- declared exchanges;
- promises;
- grievance acknowledgment if used;
- joining tune and shared departure.

This order makes difference visible before joining. It requires more space and two legible approach routes.

### The compact-first order

- couple enters together;
- public consent;
- Mera’s concise compact summary;
- household and name affirmations;
- exchanges;
- personal promises;
- departure.

This order is shorter and adapts well to rain. It can feel overly administrative unless music, objects, and family roles remain personal.

### The threshold order

- both households gather on their chosen footing;
- Tomas enters the boat and is received;
- Neris crosses beneath the Vale canopy or accepts another lineage act;
- both state retained standing and names;
- promises and exchange occur at the shared threshold;
- both leave by a route belonging to neither household alone.

This order gives family acts strong visibility. It should be used only if Tomas and Neris accept the sequence and no gift becomes a condition of marriage.

## Shrine complications

If the workshop floats free of the wedding, introduce one complication:

1. **The applauded sentence.** A family member hears one draft line and begins repeating it through town before the couple accepts it. The witnesses must correct the rumor without publicly humiliating the source.
2. **The symmetrical folio.** A junior copyist has prepared matching surname and byname spaces. Neris objects to the replacement surname; Tomas objects to a ceremonial river name invented for him. The form, not the people, must change.
3. **The wrong precedent.** Aunt Sal finds the Greybank tablet’s opening blessing and cites it as proof that the whole compact succeeded. Reading the reverse exposes the liability dispute.
4. **The private promise.** Tomas or Neris offers a beautiful personal line that the other loves but does not want entered as enforceable duty. Ansel helps preserve it outside the compact.
5. **The impossible pronunciation.** A well-meaning announcer cannot manage Reed-in-Rain under pressure. Neris decides whether to coach, authorize a short form, appoint another announcer, or omit the congregational response.
6. **The missing marker.** A child carries away one of the six wooden markers. The party must identify which function has vanished from the current plan rather than merely replacing the block.

## If the shrine is skipped or reached late

The six functions do not belong to the shrine. Mera or another competent reader can conduct them in ordinary language: separate and public consent, accepted household standing, current names, declared exchange, paraphrasable promises, and chosen departure. Use no poetic sentence that either principal cannot explain. No object completes a function by appearing.

Without Ansel and the archive, the witnesses should make fewer claims about precedent and use a plainer record. They may preserve an open clause without citing an earlier compact. Neris's name chain remains possible because the House can record stable identity and current answered name; the shrine is useful corroboration, not exclusive authority.

If a late archive tablet or officiant's concern changes a material term, reopen only that term. A precedent may offer a better form but cannot overrule the principals. Ansel may decline wording he cannot intelligibly read or archive; the ceremony can continue without shrine officiation, and the record should say so.

## Failure forward

Ansel may reject one vow, one name entry, or one public act while accepting the rest. The couple may use fewer promises, remove an object, reserve a clause, change the ceremony order, choose a private exchange, postpone, or proceed without his officiation.

Do not treat simplification as failure. Failure occurs when a beautiful form conceals a duty or when public momentum substitutes for present choice. Even then, the remedy is a pause, a narrower statement, a visible boundary, or honest refusal—not a revelation that the couple unknowingly violated an ancient taboo.
