# Vale Mill and Hearth

**Role:** necessary  
**Tags:** family, inheritance, heirlooms, mill

At the mill, flour, wages, a water lease, a white veil, a handstone, and a child's chalked shift board force welcome into exact terms.

## Opening View

Before anyone can say marriage, the mill says work. The floor shudders; flour drifts through the window bars; water shoulders the wheel around once more. Agnes Vale spreads a white veil across sacks marked by three generations of the same hand. Mara does not sit. She sets down a deed, a wage book, a water lease, care instructions, and a ring of new keys, one by one. “Tell me,” she says, “which of these does a wedding carry?”

## Encounter Material

## Situation
The Vale mill is a workplace before it is a family emblem. The wheelhouse shakes flour from the rafters. The bake room begins before dawn. Six hired workers and four tenant households order their weeks around the sluice, the stones, and Mara Vale's account books. The Vales own the buildings and machinery; the right to divert Cresswater lives in a renewable Concord lease with different signatures and remedies.

The household is preparing the wedding through things it knows how to prepare. Benches have been borrowed. Wheat and red thread mark every lintel. The eastern rooms have been scrubbed and aired. Agnes has unfolded the white veil. Mara has set deed, wage book, lease, and care instructions on separate corners of the same table. Each act is generous. Each also answers a question Tomas and Neris have not finished asking.

At the mill, every promise must survive a working week. Who takes freeze watch? Who tells customers when spawning closure stops the wheel? Where do Neris's current ledgers live? Who may spend household money, halt machinery, assign a worker, or promise a room? What happens when river duty and a profitable grain run call at the same hour? Welcome becomes keys, shifts, signatures, and somebody awake beside the wheel.

## Agnes Vale and the veil
Agnes lays the white flax veil over the grain sacks and keeps smoothing one old crease. She wore it at her wedding and later placed it over Perrin's body after the flood. To the Vales, joy and mourning may travel in the same heirloom. To Neris's kin, cloth over the face can mean breath withheld and a person removed from current.

Agnes wants Neris to receive the best-kept object of the Vale women and to speak Perrin's name before the river guests. Fear makes that offer sound larger than she intends. The cloth can become canopy, compact wrapping, threshold lining, an act begun with Tomas and opened by Neris, or a private memorial kept at home.

Ask Agnes what she is giving. Ask Neris what use she can receive. If no use carries both answers, the veil stays folded without turning refusal into insult.

## Mara Vale and the mill
Mara keeps moving between the table and the wheelhouse. Each time somebody says *welcome*, she puts a different book beneath the word: wages, debt, the water lease, Elsin's care instructions, next week's shifts. The marriage changes the mill even if no deed moves, because Tomas has been covering extra wheel work since Mara's husband died.

She needs notice when river duty takes Tomas downstream, authority named before a spawning closure interrupts a profitable run, and a fallback when both principals are absent. She offers Neris keys, equal domestic standing in the eastern rooms, household purchasing, a worker-council seat, and water-compliance work. She also recognizes the willow house as a continuing dwelling. None of those offers transfers wages, debt, succession, or the whole mill.

Asked whom she trusts, Mara says, “Neris when Neris speaks for herself. I am still learning which of her mother's promises arrive wearing Neris's name.”

## Elsin Vale is not an inheritance token

**Elsin Vale** is twelve, carries chalk behind one ear, and has memorized the sluice ratios. Adults point to her whenever they want succession to sound settled. She likes mill work. She may inherit it, manage it, or leave it. The compact must protect the place she has without turning a child's present answer into a life sentence.

Elsin can show the witnesses four separate ledgers:

- the deed book for buildings and machinery;
- the wage and debt accounts;
- the Concord water lease;
- and Mara's private care instructions naming who should house Elsin if Mara dies while she is still young.

The last ledger matters. It names Tomas first, Agnes second, and two unrelated neighbors after them. It does not award Elsin to “the Vale household” as an abstract body. Mara chose particular people and may revise the list. This provides a model for future care: duties belong to named people who accept them, not automatically to whichever household claims the stronger custom.

Elsin can concede the ceremonial phrase “Neris keeps the wheel” if everyone states that it means water accountability rather than succession. She is delighted by the idea of Neris teaching her to read current marks. She will not stand silently in the ceremony as proof that succession is solved, and she will correct anyone who says the mill is already hers.

A witness who asks Elsin what she fears receives a practical answer: “That they will promise each other every hour they have, and Mara will put my name in the empty shifts.”

## The wheel, lease, and four kinds of authority

The mill's human succession is simpler than rumor suggests. Tomas and Mara jointly operate it. Mara owns a larger share of the buildings under their parents' deed; Tomas owns the wheel machinery and part of the grain store. Elsin is a likely future successor but no present owner. The river lease belongs to the operating mill and must be renewed by its named managers with the consent of the mixed water hearing.

Workers are nevertheless worried. **Hobb Fen**, the senior mill hand, has heard that river spouses claim whatever turns in water. **Lysa Cart**, who delivers flour, has heard the opposite: that Neris will be required to stand aside while Tomas's kin use her name to renew the lease forever. Both rumors convert a marriage into a property seizure because nobody has stated the boundaries aloud.

Keep four kinds of authority separate:

1. **Property:** ownership of buildings, machinery, stored grain, and debt remains where the deed and accounts place it.
2. **Operation:** Tomas and Mara continue assigning shifts, wages, orders, and repairs unless they separately admit another manager.
3. **Household:** Tomas and Neris share authority over their dwelling, food, guests, care, and purchases without that authority extending automatically to the whole mill.
4. **Water compliance:** Neris may issue or relay closures and warnings in her current-keeper capacity; Tomas accepts specified duties under adoption and lease.

Neris can therefore be a full member of the domestic household, an acknowledged water correspondent, and a respected participant in worker councils without becoming owner, debtor, universal manager, or living embodiment of the lease.

## Tomas under ordinary pressure

Question Tomas while something needs repair. Split a grain sack, stick a gate, or send a worker for next week's shifts. He solves the immediate problem first. Then he says, “We can manage,” by which he means that he will quietly absorb the collision.

Press him gently on concrete collisions:

- If a spawning closure begins during a profitable milling run, he will stop the wheel once the signal and threshold are verified.
- If the flood signal sounds, he will open the lower sluice, ring the bell, and help clear workers and tenants before protecting machinery.
- If Neris must remain beneath the willows for a week, he will not treat her absence as withdrawal from the marriage.
- If both river duty and mill duty require him, he wants Mara or the named shift leader to hold the mill while he fulfills the warning duty.
- He will not promise to enter deep floodwater, leave vulnerable workers without warning, or obey an unnamed messenger who cannot identify the signal.

Tomas need not choose Neris against his family. He must learn that “I will take care of it” often means Mara, Neris, or a future Tomas will pay. Make him name who acts when he cannot.

## Residence, rooms, and care

The eastern range has two bedrooms, a small common room, a door into the mill yard, and stone steps descending to the runnel. Tomas has repaired the steps for Neris. He has not noticed that every storage chest is labeled “Vale East,” every guest assumes the willow house is being left behind, and Agnes has begun planning which river cousins may visit rather than recognizing them as Neris's household.

Separate welcome from absorption:

- Neris receives keys and equal domestic authority in the eastern rooms.
- Her current ledgers and river tools have dedicated space rather than being treated as temporary belongings.
- The willow house remains a recognized dwelling for Neris and is available to Tomas by its household rules.
- No count of nights determines loyalty, inheritance, or abandonment.
- If either partner is incapacitated, prior directions and the other partner's voice govern where care occurs; neither family may simply remove its relative.
- If one partner dies, the survivor retains the eastern rooms for an agreed period or for life, depending on the couple's choice, without acquiring the dead partner's unrelated property by default.

Mara is open to a lifetime dwelling right for Neris if Tomas dies, provided it is tied to the eastern rooms and domestic garden rather than the entire mill. Serein will later ask for an equivalent assurance that Tomas may receive care beneath the willows without surrendering himself to river custody.

## The stone gift

Mara has commissioned a small ceremonial millstone as the Vale household's principal gift. To mortals, a stone with a clean central eye means steadiness, labor, and a household fed by repeated work. To river folk, a pierced stone presented to a bride can mean that her course is fixed and her voice made to turn another household's machinery.

Mara will be wounded if told that the gift is simply offensive. She chose it because she respects Neris's practical work. She is open to altering its use: the stone might become the base of the public water bowl, a table at the shared threshold, or one half of a paired gift whose other half moves freely.

Mara will not present the stone as a title to Neris's labor or as proof that Neris “keeps the wheel” in every sense. If the witnesses distinguish the meanings well, Mara may ask Neris to choose its public use herself.

Mara's workshop inventory already provides the bones of an honest declaration. It names her as owner and giver, the retired millstone as source, the mason as maker, the shared water bowl as the proposed use, and return to Mara if Neris refuses it. The line for permitted alteration is still blank. Filling that line with Neris is more useful than asking either household what a pierced stone means in general.

## Productive scenes

- Tomas shows the witnesses the eastern rooms and admits that he has promised each family that “nothing important will change.”
- Mara produces the four ledgers and asks whether Neris will sign as household member, water representative, both, or neither.
- Agnes asks one adventurer to tell her, plainly, whether the river family considers Tomas beneath them.
- Elsin demonstrates how the mill can stop without closing the river channel, a practical image for separating household continuity from control of the water.
- Hobb asks whether he takes a flood order from Neris as Tomas's wife or as a current-keeper; the correct answer may be the latter, and the distinction matters.
- A private conversation with Tomas reveals that he is willing to postpone but dreads telling Agnes because he believes she will hear postponement as a verdict on Perrin.

## Household outcomes

Leave the mill with positions, not a capitulation.

- Agnes may offer the veil in transformed use, keep it while accepting another remembrance, or attend without publicly recognizing the new household.
- Mara may grant Neris domestic authority and a water role while keeping ownership and succession separate.
- Elsin may join the water-reading lesson, refuse ceremonial use as “heir,” or offer the care ledger as a model.
- Tomas may accept exact flood duties, revise his work schedule, and publicly acknowledge two homes even if he still prefers to call the eastern rooms the household's center.

## If the mill is skipped or reached late

The wedding does not wait behind the mill door. If the witnesses never visit, Tomas can still state his personal consent, names, residence intention, and promises. What they cannot presume is Vale household recognition, use of Agnes's veil, Mara's operational commitment, a change in mill authority, or any transfer of property, debt, or succession.

At joining light, ask Tomas which rooms, goods, and duties he personally controls. Anything beyond that remains open unless the actual authority holder answers. The eastern rooms may be recorded as a dwelling Tomas offers within his authority; they do not become proof that Mara accepted Neris into mill management or that the Vale deed changed. The ceremony may omit the veil, use it only if Agnes has separately released it, and state no common Vale household standing.

If the encounter is reached after plans have hardened, bring only the affected choices back for correction. Mara may narrow an operational clause without reopening names or feast arrangements. Agnes may withdraw the veil without undoing Neris's consent. Elsin's observation may correct how adults describe care without making her responsible for deciding inheritance. Late mill evidence changes the smallest relevant scope.

## Failure forward

A quarrel here gives the wedding sharper edges. Agnes may withhold the veil; workers may demand lease language; Mara may keep the stone; Elsin may refuse ceremonial use; Tomas may contradict his family in public. Carry the cost forward. Do not smooth it away.

If the party accepts vague reassurance, the mill remains operational but the unresolved labor appears later: an unstaffed shift, a disputed closure order, or a witness unable to paraphrase who acts during a flood. Make the cost visible before the ceremony so the couple can revise rather than be ambushed afterward.

## Agnes’s seat and the guest register

When Pella’s guest sheet reaches the mill, somebody has written “best elder place” beside Agnes’s name. Agnes strikes it out. What she needs is a stable chair, low background noise, and a clear view of the speaker’s face. At the wheelhouse she hears almost nothing; at a ceremonial head table beside musicians she would miss the vows while appearing honored.

Agnes’s correction is a useful hospitality clue. Her need belongs to Agnes, not to “mortal elders.” Aunt Sal walks farther and hears better but wants a handrail on wet ground. Elsin needs no special seat and resents being placed with children too young to follow the ceremony. The Vale household can confirm names and needs, but each affected person should answer where possible.
